# Chartered Monopolies and the Investor’s Dilemma: The VOC and the EIC, 1725–1750

## The Dilemma Between Investments in the VOC and the EIC

My dear ancestor, it seemed appropriate to take the time to write you this letter before you make what could be a great investment, or a catastrophic mistake.
Before you make your decision, read this letter through and through: I am from the future, and I have great insight into this dilemma. In 1725, the VOC appeared to be the safer investment,
with an average dividend of about 18%, a 120-year history, and the reputation of one of the world's largest trading companies. The EIC looked more politicized and riskier. By 1750, however, those roles had reversed. Why?

The EIC is the better financial bet because of its capital market, not because it was morally superior — both were chartered monopolies. The VOC, however, was still in steady decline; its high dividend was a
symptom of that decline.

Let's look more closely at the EIC. Remember the 18% dividend I mentioned? The biggest factor behind it is that the dividend was funded by borrowing, which kept the company afloat. This comes straight from
Zheng, "Reasons for the Decline of the Dutch East India Company" (2020), p. 113. In addition, the EIC's bond coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and to 3% in 1737.
These are two reasons the EIC could make more sense, but let's compare the two fortunes. The VOC's shares declined between 1721 and 1763. Why? According to Taylor (2026, 34), “Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands
all hit new lows at the beginning of 1762. There were no major bull markets for four decades as most investors chose safe government bonds over risky equities.”

## Two Companies, Two Constitutions

The VOC was a state-like monopoly, which is why it may have appeared to be the safer option. The 1602 octrooi granted a 21-year monopoly east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan (Octrooi, art. XXXIV).
Along with granting an entire monopoly, it also granted sovereign powers: the right to make treaties, build forts, appoint governors, raise troops, and administer courts (art. XXXV). As you can imagine, this also granted the power to wage war.
Now those powers came with a high cost: by 1750 the VOC had about 35,000 employees, most of them soldiers (Zheng, p. 110).
Van Imhoff (pp. 3–4), who later became Governor-General, named "the too great extent of its possessions" as the first cause of decline. You can see why this could create problems eventually. Let's
move on to the EIC.

The EIC was also a chartered corporation. Do not mistake this: it was a monopoly too, but it operated within a parliamentary system and a deep London capital market. Its East India bonds were nominally
short-term but rolled over into long-term debt with embedded call and put options (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371). The EIC's coupon fell to 4%
in 1728 and 3% in 1737. While the VOC was in steady decline and grew riskier over time, the EIC became the more tempting option. In addition, shareholders had a public voice: the 1727
pamphlet defends the directors in print and asks, "What is the only point to the Proprietors, any Loss to the Company, by the misconduct of the Directors?" (1727, p. 25). However, the 1730 pamphlet calls a chartered joint-stock company "to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly" (1730, p. vi).


## The Fortunes of the Two Companies, 1725–1750

The VOC had a high-cost, low-growth model. Salary costs rose from 8,887,198 guilders in 1705–07 to 12,289,055 guilders in 1766 (Zheng, p. 110). In addition, there was corruption within the VOC as well as smuggling. This
undermined the spice, coffee, and opium monopolies (Zheng, p. 111). The trade around this time shifted from spices and pepper to cotton and tea; the VOC, however, was slow to adapt. There was also the dividend trap:
"Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist" (Zheng, p. 113). However, a high dividend funded by debt is a transfer from future shareholders
to current ones, and it makes the company fragile.

The EIC, however, had cheaper capital and a deeper market. The East India bond coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737 (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371).
In addition, the London market was integrated and could price sophisticated options (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 367), which lowered the EIC's cost of capital. The EIC's shareholders debated governance in print (1727, pp. 5, 22, 25).
VOC shares, by contrast, were book-entry claims, and trading volume collapsed after 1720; liquidity was "critically low" for nearly a decade (Phung, pp. 4, 17).
On the one hand, these are real advantages over the VOC. You would have a lower cost of capital, a deeper market, and more information and voice. These are not necessarily moral advantages.


## Which Company Would I Invest In?

What is my case for the EIC?
If you invest in the EIC, you would have cheaper debt (again, 3–4% coupons), a liquid London market, parliamentary accountability, a diversified trade portfolio (textiles, tea), and a
public sphere for shareholders. An investor in 1725 was buying a stream of dividends and a claim on a trading empire. The EIC's stream was less fragile because its capital costs were lower
and its market was deeper. The VOC's stream looked richer (18%), but it was funded by borrowing and backed by an illiquid share. There were risks to the EIC, of course: it was still
exposed to war, political risk, and the secular bear market. The recommendation is relative, not absolute. The EIC was still a monopoly, and the financial system that gave it cheap capital was entangled with the South Sea Company’s slave
trade (Price and Whatley, p. 4). It was not morally superior to the VOC.

What is my case against the VOC?
If you invest in the VOC, you would have high fixed costs, corruption, falling spice rents, slow adaptation to tea and textiles, illiquid shares, and dividends funded by debt. A contrarian might be tempted
by the high yield, but there was no catalyst for recovery. Van Imhoff, who later became Governor-General, was diagnosing decline in 1741. The 18% dividend was compensation for risk, not a free lunch. If the VOC had reformed
its cost structure and restored its monopoly rents, the calculation would change. It did not.


## When Would I Cash Out, 1745–1750?

I would say the late 1740s were the least-bad window to cash out, as there was no true peak. The War of the Austrian Succession ended with the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. In January 1749, EIC
proprietors were voting on the Company's capital structure; the ballot and the Three Letters show an active, informed shareholder body (1749, title page). The capital stock was £3.2 million (p. 22).
An investor who wanted to sell had a better chance of doing so in 1748–1749 than in the thin market of the 1720s or the war years after 1756. The window is about liquidity and risk reduction, not
maximizing the sale price.

Why not wait for a peak? Taylor shows that French and Dutch shares declined between 1721 and 1763, and that markets in England, France, and the Netherlands hit new lows at the beginning of 1762 (Taylor, p. 34).
The 1720–1763 period was a secular bear market: UK stock returns averaged 2.94% a year, Dutch returns 1.18% (Taylor, p. 33). Waiting for a peak would have meant holding through the Seven Years' War and the
1762 lows. The rational move was to exit into the post-war liquidity, not to hold for a recovery that the data do not show.

## Conclusion

The EIC was the better financial bet because of its capital market, not its morals; the late 1740s were the least-bad exit; the ethical alternative was to refuse the structure.
The VOC and the EIC were among the first modern corporations, and the questions they raise — how to price political risk, how to judge a company's ethics, when to exit — are still with us.
Amsterdam's market was a confusion of confusions (De la Vega, 1688); the investor's task was to see through the confusion without pretending the choice was clean. Which choice would you make?
