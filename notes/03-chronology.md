# Chronology

**How to use this file.** The paper covers 1725–1750, but the argument depends on earlier institutional choices and later consequences. Dates in **bold** are the ones most likely to appear in the paper. Where a date is disputed, I say so.

---

## Before 1725: the institutional setup

- **1600, 31 December — The English East India Company is chartered** by Elizabeth I. It is a joint-stock company with a monopoly on English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope. Unlike the VOC, it is not a federal state; it is a chartered corporation whose privileges are renewed and renegotiated by Parliament and the Crown.
- **1602, 20 March — The VOC receives its octrooi** from the States General. The charter creates a 21-year monopoly on Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan, and it grants the Company sovereign-like powers: treaties, forts, troops, courts, and war. The Company is organized as six chambers (Amsterdam, Zeeland, Rotterdam, Delft, Hoorn, Enkhuizen), governed by the Heren XVII. Amsterdam is the largest chamber.
- **1609 — The Bank of Amsterdam (Amsterdamsche Wisselbank) is founded.** It accepts bullion and coin, issues receipts, and supports the Amsterdam market. Rousseau dates its start to 1602; the conventional date is 1609. Use 1609 if you mention it, or avoid the date.
- **1619 — Batavia is founded** as the VOC’s Asian headquarters. It becomes the center of the Company’s intra-Asian trade and administration.
- **1621 — The Banda genocide.** The VOC conquers the Banda islands and kills or enslaves much of the population to secure the nutmeg monopoly. The Nationaal Archief’s page on the octrooi identifies this as one of the consequences of the charter’s sovereign powers.
- **1652–54 — First Anglo-Dutch War.** The Navigation Acts and commercial rivalry lead to war. The English and Dutch are direct competitors in the East India trade.
- **1665–67 — Second Anglo-Dutch War.** The Dutch briefly take Suriname; the English take New Netherland. The commercial rivalry continues.
- **1672–74 — Third Anglo-Dutch War and the Rampjaar.** The Dutch Republic is attacked by England, France, and two German states. The financial strain reinforces the importance of credible public debt.
- **1688 — Joseph de la Vega publishes *Confusión de Confusiones*** in Amsterdam, and William of Orange lands in England. De la Vega’s book is the classic contemporary description of the Amsterdam bourse; the Glorious Revolution creates the political settlement that underpins the British financial revolution.
- **1693–1701 — Pieter van Dam writes *Beschryvinge van de Oostindische Compagnie***, the VOC’s internal description of its governance and trade. It is a modern scholarly edition, not a period print.
- **1694 — The Bank of England is founded.** It is a joint-stock bank that lends to the government; it becomes the center of the London financial market.
- **1698 — John Castaing begins *The Course of the Exchange***, a printed price current for stocks, bonds, and exchange rates. It is the ancestor of the modern financial press. I could not find a free digitized run for 1725–50; it is behind library databases.
- **1701 — The Act of Settlement** settles the Protestant succession and reinforces parliamentary control over the monarchy. It is part of the institutional background to the British financial revolution.
- **1711 — The South Sea Company is chartered.** Parliament creates it to convert illiquid short-term public debt into tradable equity. It is the centerpiece of the British financial revolution.
- **1713 — The Peace of Utrecht** ends the War of the Spanish Succession. The South Sea Company receives the Asiento, the monopoly right to supply enslaved Africans to the Spanish Empire (1713–1743).
- **1717 — Britain moves to the gold standard** at the mint price set by Isaac Newton. The stable currency lowers the risk of long-term lending.
- **1719–1720 — The South Sea Bubble, the Mississippi Bubble, and the Dutch bubble.** Stock prices in London, Paris, and Amsterdam rise and collapse. The bubble scars a generation of investors and contributes to the long equity bear market of 1720–1763.
- **1720 — The Bubble Act** restricts the formation of joint-stock companies without a charter. The Dutch market also contracts; Phung finds a structural break in VOC trading volume after 1720.

## 1725–1750: the period under study

- **1723 — Neal’s VOC share-price series begins.** It runs from 1723 to 1794. This is the earliest continuous price series that covers the paper’s window.
- **1727 — *A Letter to the Chairman of the East-India Company* is printed.** A proprietor defends the directors against a group of memorialists who accuse them of self-dealing in the Company’s sales. The pamphlet shows that EIC shareholders had a public voice and a concept of accountability.
- **1728 — The India bond coupon is reduced to 4%.** Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret date the reduction to 1728, citing Dickson. It is evidence that the EIC’s credit is improving.
- **1730 — *A Collection of Papers Relating to the East India Trade* is printed.** The introduction argues that a chartered joint-stock company with an exclusive trade is “to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly.” It is the best period source for the free-trade critique of the EIC.
- **1737 — The India bond coupon is reduced to 3%.** The EIC can borrow at 3% on a callable/putable bond. The London market is deep and integrated.
- **1739 — The War of Jenkins’ Ear** begins between Britain and Spain. It is a precursor to the wider War of the Austrian Succession.
- **1740–1748 — The War of the Austrian Succession.** Britain and France fight in Europe and in India. The war strains public finances and disrupts trade. The EIC’s military role in India grows.
- **1741 — Van Imhoff writes his *Considérations*.** The Governor-General diagnoses three causes of VOC decline: over-extended possessions, falling Eastern goods prices in Europe, and demoralized or corrupt employees. The French edition is printed in 1763.
- **1743 — Van Imhoff becomes Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies** (1743–1750). He attempts reforms, but the Company’s structural problems persist.
- **1745 — The Jacobite rising.** Charles Edward Stuart’s rebellion unsettles British politics and financial markets. The Bank of England faces a run; the episode shows how political risk could affect the London market.
- **1748 — The Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle** ends the War of the Austrian Succession. The peace is a relief, but it does not restore the pre-war equity market. For an investor, 1748–49 is a liquidity window, not a peak.
- **1749, 3 January — The EIC proprietors’ ballot.** The proprietors vote on whether to accept the government’s proposal to reduce the interest on £3.2 million of government debt from 4% to 3.5% (1750) and then 3% (1757). *Three Letters to the Proprietors* is printed for the ballot. This is the closest period source to the cash-out decision.
- **1750 — Van Imhoff dies in Batavia.** The EIC’s India bond coupon is at 3%; the Company’s capital stock is £3.2 million. The VOC’s debt burden is growing.

## After 1750: the consequences

- **1751–1752 — Pelham’s conversion creates the 3% Consols.** The British government consolidates its debt at a lower interest rate. The London market becomes even more liquid and government debt becomes the benchmark safe asset.
- **1756–1763 — The Seven Years’ War.** Britain and France fight a global war. The EIC’s territorial power in India expands after the Battle of Plassey (1757). The war is expensive and disruptive for trade.
- **1762 — Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands hit new lows.** Taylor dates the bottom of the long bear market to the beginning of 1762. An investor who held through the 1750s would have suffered.
- **1763 — The Treaty of Paris** ends the Seven Years’ War. The VOC’s debt load continues to grow; the Company is increasingly dependent on borrowed money.
- **1780–1784 — The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War.** The VOC’s trade collapses; sales fall from about 21 million guilders to less than 6 million in 1781–82. The Company is effectively bankrupt.
- **1799 — The VOC is dissolved.** Its territories are taken over by the Dutch state. The Company that helped create the first modern securities market ends as a state-owned ruin.

## How the chronology supports the argument

1. **The institutional contrast is old.** The VOC was a state-like monopoly from 1602; the EIC was a chartered corporation embedded in a parliamentary system. By 1725, the EIC had access to a deeper, cheaper capital market.
2. **The 1720 bubble is the turning point.** After 1720, the VOC’s share market thins, its dividends are funded by debt, and its long-run returns lag. The EIC’s India bonds become cheaper and more flexible.
3. **The late 1740s are a window, not a peak.** The Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) and the 1749 ballot create a moment of relative liquidity. Taylor’s long-run series shows that the secular trend was down, and that the market hit new lows in 1762. The correct recommendation is to exit in the late 1740s, before the Seven Years’ War and the 1762 lows — not to wait for a peak that never came.
4. **The ethical stakes are structural.** The VOC’s charter authorized conquest, slavery, and genocide. The British financial revolution, which made the EIC’s cheap capital possible, was entangled with the South Sea Company’s slave trade. The ethical alternative is not to pick the nicer monopoly; it is to refuse the structure.
