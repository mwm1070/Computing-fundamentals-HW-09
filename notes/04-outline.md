# Paragraph-by-Paragraph Outline

**Target:** 1,400 words in the body, before the bibliography. The assignment allows 1,000–1,500; 1,400 gives you room to make the argument without padding. The bibliography is separate and does not count.

**Working thesis:** The EIC was the better *financial* investment in 1725–1750, not because it was ethically superior, but because it had cheaper and more flexible capital, a more liquid market, and a governing structure that could borrow at 3–4% while the VOC was over-extended, illiquid, and paying dividends out of borrowed money. The best exit from EIC stock was the late 1740s — a **least-bad window**, not a peak. The ethical alternative was to refuse both chartered monopolies and to direct capital toward transparent, non-coercive enterprise, while using shareholder voice to demand reform.

**A note on the thesis.** Do not write “the EIC was good.” It was not. Write “the EIC was the better of two bad investments.” That distinction is the paper’s intellectual spine. It lets you answer the investment question without pretending that the EIC was morally clean.

## Introduction (about 150 words)

**Purpose:** Define the puzzle, state the thesis, and preview the structure.

**Time-travel frame:** You arrive in 1725 with capital and a mandate to exit by 1750. The paper is your investment memo: which company, when to sell, and what to do instead. Use the frame in the first and last paragraphs; do not turn every sentence into a time-travel conceit.

**What to do:**
1. Open with a concrete contrast: in 1725, the VOC looked like the safer investment — it had paid an average dividend of about 18%, it was the world’s largest trading company, and it had a 120-year history. The EIC looked like the riskier, newer, more politicized alternative. By 1750, the positions had reversed. Why?
2. State the thesis: the EIC was the better financial bet because of its capital market, not its morality. The VOC’s high dividend was a symptom of decline, not strength.
3. Preview the four moves: (a) the constitutional differences, (b) the fortunes of the two companies in 1725–50, (c) the investment decision, (d) the cash-out window and the ethical alternative.

**Evidence to use:**
- Zheng, p. 113: the 18% average dividend was funded by borrowing.
- Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371: EIC India bond coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737.
- Taylor, p. 34: VOC shares declined between 1721 and 1763.

**Transition:** “To see why the EIC won, we need to start with the constitutions that shaped the two companies’ access to capital.”

## Section 1: Two companies, two constitutions (about 250 words)

**Purpose:** Explain the institutional differences that produced different costs of capital.

**Paragraph 1.1 — The VOC as a state-like monopoly (about 125 words).**
- Topic sentence: The VOC was not simply a firm; it was an arm of the Dutch state in Asia.
- Evidence: the 1602 octrooi granted a 21-year monopoly east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan, on pain of forfeiture of ships and goods (Octrooi, art. XXXIV). It also granted sovereign powers: treaties, forts, governors, troops, courts, and war (art. XXXV).
- Analysis: those powers were expensive. The Company carried the overhead of a colonial government — about 35,000 employees by 1750, most of them soldiers (Zheng, p. 110). Its fixed costs were structural, not temporary.
- Primary voice: Van Imhoff, the Governor-General, identified “the too-great extent of its possessions” as the first cause of decline (Van Imhoff, pp. 3–4).
- Transition: The EIC was also a monopoly, but its constitution was different.

**Paragraph 1.2 — The EIC as a chartered corporation (about 125 words).**
- Topic sentence: The EIC was a chartered monopoly too, but it was embedded in a parliamentary system and a deep London capital market.
- Evidence: the EIC’s India bonds were nominally short-term but rolled over into long-term debt with embedded call and put options (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371). The coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737 (p. 371).
- Analysis: the EIC could borrow cheaply and flexibly because investors trusted the paper and the market was liquid. The Company’s shareholders also had a public voice: the 1727 pamphlet defends the directors in print and asks “what is the only point to the Proprietors” (1727, p. 25).
- Caveat: the EIC was still a monopoly. The 1730 pamphlet calls a chartered joint-stock company “to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly” (1730, p. vi).
- Transition: Constitutions matter because they shape costs. The next section shows how those costs played out between 1725 and 1750.

**Do not:**
- Do not say the EIC was a free market. It was not.
- Do not say the VOC was “feudal” or “backward” without evidence. It was a sophisticated financial institution; the problem was its cost structure and its shrinking rents.

## Section 2: The fortunes of the two companies, 1725–1750 (about 300 words)

**Purpose:** Show that the VOC’s apparent strength was fragile and the EIC’s apparent weakness was relative.

**Paragraph 2.1 — The VOC’s high-cost, low-growth model (about 150 words).**
- Topic sentence: The VOC’s headline dividend concealed a deteriorating business.
- Evidence: the Company’s salary costs rose from 8,887,198 guilders in 1705–07 to 12,289,055 in 1766 (Zheng, p. 110). Corruption was systemic; smuggling undermined the spice, coffee, and opium monopolies (p. 111). The trade shifted from spices and pepper to cotton and tea, and the VOC was slow to adapt (p. 112).
- The dividend trap: “Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist” (p. 113).
- Analysis: a high dividend funded by debt is not a sign of health. It is a transfer from future shareholders to current ones, and it makes the Company fragile.
- Transition: The EIC faced the same trade shift, but it had cheaper capital and a more flexible commercial system.

**Paragraph 2.2 — The EIC’s cheaper capital and deeper market (about 150 words).**
- Topic sentence: The EIC’s advantage was not that it was more virtuous; it was that it could borrow more cheaply.
- Evidence: the India bond coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737 (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371). The London market was integrated and capable of pricing sophisticated options (p. 367). The EIC’s shareholders debated governance in print (1727, pp. 5, 22, 25).
- Contrast: the VOC’s shares were book-entry claims, and trading volume collapsed after 1720; liquidity remained “critically low” for nearly a decade (Phung, Abstract, p. 4; p. 17).
- Analysis: the EIC’s cost of capital was lower, its market was deeper, and its shareholders had more information and more voice. Those are investment advantages, not moral ones.
- Transition: If you were allocating capital in 1725, the choice is becoming clear — but the ethical question remains.

**Do not:**
- Do not say the VOC “collapsed” in 1725–50. It did not. It declined relative to the EIC and accumulated debt.
- Do not say the EIC “won” the trade war. It won the financial comparison, not every commercial battle.

## Section 3: Which would you invest in? (about 250 words)

**Purpose:** Answer the assignment’s investment question directly.

**Paragraph 3.1 — The case for the EIC (about 150 words).**
- Topic sentence: On financial grounds, the EIC was the better investment in 1725 — but only as the less bad of two chartered monopolies.
- Evidence: cheaper debt (3–4% coupons), a liquid London market, parliamentary accountability, a diversified trade portfolio (textiles, tea), and a shareholder public sphere.
- Analysis: an investor in 1725 was buying a stream of dividends and a claim on a trading empire. The EIC’s stream was more secure because its capital costs were lower and its market was deeper. The VOC’s stream looked richer (18%) but was funded by borrowing and backed by an illiquid share.
- Risk: the EIC was still exposed to war, political risk, and the secular bear market. The recommendation is relative, not absolute.

**Paragraph 3.2 — The case against the VOC (about 100 words).**
- Topic sentence: The VOC was not a value play; it was a value trap.
- Evidence: high fixed costs, corruption, falling spice rents, slow adaptation to tea and textiles, illiquid shares, and dividends funded by debt.
- Analysis: a contrarian investor might be tempted by the high yield, but there was no catalyst for recovery. The Company’s own Governor-General was diagnosing decline in 1741. The 18% dividend was compensation for risk, not a free lunch.
- Caveat: if the VOC had reformed its cost structure and restored its monopoly rents, the calculation would change. It did not.

**Do not:**
- Do not recommend the EIC without acknowledging its monopoly and slavery links.
- Do not use the word “safe.” Neither investment was safe.

## Section 4: When would you cash out, 1745–1750? (about 250 words)

**Purpose:** Answer the timing question and refute the old paper’s “peak” claim.

**Paragraph 4.1 — The late 1740s as a least-bad window (about 150 words).**
- Topic sentence: The best exit window was the late 1740s — roughly 1748–49 — not because it was a peak, but because it was a moment of relative liquidity and political relief.
- Evidence: the War of the Austrian Succession ended with the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. In January 1749, EIC proprietors were voting on the Company’s capital structure; the ballot and the *Three Letters* show an active, informed shareholder body (1749, title page, p. 22). The Company’s capital stock was £3.2 million (p. 22).
- Analysis: an investor who wanted to sell had a better chance of doing so in 1748–49 than in the thin market of the 1720s or the war years of the 1750s. The window is about liquidity and risk reduction, not maximizing the sale price.

**Paragraph 4.2 — Why not wait for a peak? (about 100 words).**
- Topic sentence: There was no post-war peak to wait for.
- Evidence: Taylor shows that English, French, and Dutch stocks declined between 1721 and 1763, and that markets hit new lows at the beginning of 1762 (Taylor, p. 34). The 1720–1763 period was a secular bear market: UK stock returns averaged 2.94% a year, Dutch returns 1.18% (p. 33).
- Analysis: waiting for a peak would have meant holding through the Seven Years’ War and the 1762 lows. The rational move was to exit into the post-war liquidity, not to hold for a recovery that the data do not show.
- Refutation: the old paper’s claim that 1748–50 was a “post-war peak” is not supported by the sources. Replace “peak” with “least-bad window.”

**Do not:**
- Do not invent a share price for 1748 or 1749. The sources do not give one. Talk about the window, not a number.
- Do not claim the 1749 pamphlet recommends selling shares. It is about the interest reduction on government debt.

## Section 5: The ethical alternative (about 200 words)

**Purpose:** Answer the assignment’s ethical question without turning the paper into a sermon.

**Paragraph 5.1 — The ethical problem is structural (about 100 words).**
- Topic sentence: The choice between the VOC and the EIC is not a choice between a clean company and a dirty one.
- Evidence: the VOC’s charter authorized conquest, slavery, and genocide (Octrooi, arts. XXXIV–XXXV; Nationaal Archief). The British financial revolution, which made the EIC’s cheap capital possible, was entangled with the South Sea Company’s slave trade (Price and Whatley, p. 4). The EIC was itself a chartered monopoly (1730, p. vi).
- Analysis: both companies were rent-seeking, coercive, and embedded in colonial violence. An ethical investor cannot solve the problem by picking the “nicer” monopolist.

**Paragraph 5.2 — The alternative (about 100 words).**
- Topic sentence: The ethical alternative is to refuse the structure, not to choose within it.
- Evidence: the 1730 pamphlet’s free-trade critique argues that a chartered joint-stock company is a monopoly even when Parliament sanctions it (1730, p. vi). Price and Whatley show that the financial revolution’s gains were partly built on slavery (p. 4).
- Proposal: an ethical investor in 1725–50 could (a) abstain from both chartered monopolies, (b) hold government debt or diversified private trade claims instead, and (c) use shareholder voice — where available — to demand transparency, limits on monopoly, and abolition. The point is not to find a pure investment; it is to refuse to profit from coercion without protest.
- Caveat: this is a modern ethical judgment, not a period one. Say so. The paper can argue that the eighteenth-century alternatives were constrained, but it should not pretend that abstention was easy or common.

**Do not:**
- Do not say “both sides were bad” and leave it there. The assignment asks for an alternative.
- Do not claim that government debt was ethically pure. It financed wars. The alternative is less harmful, not innocent.

## Conclusion (about 100 words)

**Purpose:** Restate the answer and raise the stakes.

**What to do:**
1. Restate the thesis in one sentence: the EIC was the better financial bet because of its capital market, not its morals; the late 1740s were the least-bad exit; the ethical alternative was to refuse the structure.
2. Zoom out: the VOC and the EIC were the first modern corporations, and the questions they raise — how to price political risk, how to judge a company’s ethics, when to exit — are still with us.
3. End with a concrete image or question, not a generic summary. For example: “The first modern stock market was a confusion of confusions; the investor’s task was to see through the confusion without pretending that the choice was clean.”

**Do not:**
- Do not introduce new evidence in the conclusion.
- Do not end with “In conclusion…” or a restatement of the prompt.
