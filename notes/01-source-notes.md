# Source Notes — VOC vs. EIC, 1725–1750

**Purpose.** These are the working notes for the paper. They are deliberately longer than the paper (the assignment asks for intermediate notes at least twice the length of the final draft). Use them as a quarry: each entry gives the citation, the type of source, the main claims, exact quotations with page numbers, how the source fits the argument, and the limitations you should acknowledge.

**Working thesis.** The English East India Company (EIC) was the better *financial* investment in 1725–1750, but not because it was ethically superior. It had cheaper and more flexible capital, a more liquid market, and a governing structure that could borrow at 3–4% while the Dutch East India Company (VOC) was over-extended, illiquid, and paying dividends out of borrowed money. The best exit from EIC stock was the late 1740s — a **least-bad window**, not a peak. The ethical alternative was to refuse both chartered monopolies and to direct capital toward transparent, non-coercive enterprise, while using shareholder voice to demand reform.

**Citation convention.** Page numbers are the printed page numbers of the source, not the PDF page numbers, unless I say otherwise. For the pamphlets, the printed page numbers are in parentheses at the top of the page. For the journal articles, the page numbers are the journal pages.

## A. Secondary sources

### 1. Zheng, “Reasons for the Decline of the Dutch East India Company” (2020)

**Citation.** Zheng, Haiyang. “Reasons for the Decline of the Dutch East India Company.” *Modern Management Forum* 4, no. 3 (2020): 110–114. https://doi.org/10.18686/mmf.v4i3.2505.

**Type.** Secondary; English; peer-reviewed-style short article. Useful as a compact statement of the standard “VOC decline” case, but it is a synthesis, not primary evidence.

**What it argues.** Zheng identifies four internal causes of VOC decline: high salary and administrative costs; lazy and corrupt employees; an increasingly backward monopolistic business model; and failure to adapt to the eighteenth-century shift from spices and pepper to cotton textiles and tea. He adds a financial cause — sustained losses from the 1690s, hidden from shareholders and masked by regular dividends — and a final blow from the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–84).

**Key claims and page references.**
- The VOC had about 7,700 employees in 1625 and about 35,000 by 1750, most of them military officers and soldiers (p. 110).
- Salary payments rose from 8,887,198 guilders in 1705–07 to 12,289,055 guilders in 1766 (p. 110).
- The Company’s monopoly required a large army and a large administrative staff, which meant heavy fixed costs (pp. 110–111).
- Corruption was systemic: in the Moluccas, one-fifth of the money paid to native chiefs for spices went to Company officials; in Java, pepper and rice prices were renegotiated at each transaction, creating rent-seeking (p. 111).
- Smuggling by officials, including governors-general, undermined the spice, coffee and opium monopolies, especially after 1750 (p. 111).
- The spice and pepper trade was replaced by cotton and tea. Pepper and spices were 75% of VOC imports around 1620, 41% by 1670, and 23% by 1700; cotton fabrics were 36% in 1670 and 55% in 1700 (p. 112).
- The VOC was the largest Chinese tea trafficker before the 1760s, but the EIC overtook it by the 1770s; the British cut costs by trading directly at Guangzhou instead of transshipping through Batavia (p. 112).
- The Company had sustained losses from about the 1690s. Directors withheld financial information from shareholders, who were “long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual.” To pay dividends regularly, “the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist” (p. 113).
- In Asia the Company’s income remained above 4 million guilders a year on average in the eighteenth century, but this was not enough to cover accumulated deficits (p. 113).
- By 1780 the Company had a loan of nearly 19 million guilders; the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War cut sales from about 21 million guilders to less than 6 million in 1781–82 (p. 113).

**Direct quotations.**
> “Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist.” (p. 113)

> “The spices and pepper trade was replaced by the cotton and tea trade.” (p. 112)

> “By the 18th century, the disadvantages of monopoly had exposed very obvious [sic]. While British private firms had shown the flexible ability to response the market in Asia, especially in India, VOC still stuck to old-fashioned, unwieldy strategy.” (p. 111)

**How to use it.** Zheng is the backbone of the “VOC was structurally weak” section. Use the 18% dividend quote to show that the headline yield was not a sign of health: it was a financing strategy that concealed losses. Use the cost and corruption figures to explain why the VOC could not match the EIC’s cheaper capital. Use the spice-to-tea shift to explain why the VOC’s old monopoly rents were disappearing.

**Limitations.** Zheng is a short synthesis and relies on older scholarship (Gaastra, Kroef, van der Korte). It is not a primary source, and some of its numbers are loosely presented (for example, the claim that Asian income “reached over 700,000 guilders per year” is inconsistent with the surrounding figures and should not be quoted). It says little about EIC finances, so it cannot by itself support the comparative investment judgment. Use it for the VOC side and pair it with Nogués-Marco for the EIC side.

### 2. Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, “East India Bonds, 1718–1763” (2007)

**Citation.** Nogués-Marco, Pilar, and Camila Vam Malle-Sabouret. “East India Bonds, 1718–1763: Early Exotic Derivatives and London Market Efficiency.” *European Review of Economic History* 11, no. 3 (2007): 367–394. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1361491607002055.

**Type.** Secondary; English; quantitative financial history. This is the best source in the set for the EIC’s cost of capital.

**What it argues.** The India bonds issued by the EIC were not simple short-term debt. Although nominally short-term, they were rolled over and became long-term bonds with embedded call and put options: the Company could redeem at par, and investors could demand repayment at par. Contemporary investors understood and priced these options. Once the options are taken into account, the London domestic market looks integrated, not inefficient. The bonds were precursors of modern hedging instruments.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The India bonds carried coupons ranging from 3 to 6 per cent, depending on the period (p. 369).
- The coupon was reduced to 4% in 1728 and to 3% in 1737 (p. 371, citing Dickson 1967, pp. 472–473).
- The bonds were equivalent to long-term bonds with embedded call and put options (pp. 371–372).
- Contemporary manuals described the bonds as payable by the Company on six months’ notice and receivable by the Company as cash in payment for goods bought at its sales (p. 371, quoting Massie 1750, pp. 25–26).
- The price of the bonds stayed close to par because the options bounded it: investors would not pay much above par, and would not sell much below par (p. 372).
- The London market was integrated across securities; investors could arbitrage between India bonds and Consols (pp. 367–372).
- The creation of this sophisticated security was a response to a parliamentary restriction that prevented the EIC from issuing long-term bonds, not simply market deregulation (p. 369).

**Direct quotations.**
> “Although the India bonds were nominally short-term debts, they were in practice rolled over and thus became equivalent to long-term bonds.” (p. 371)

> “The East India Company’s Bonds are payable [by the Company] upon six Months Notice and the Company receives them as Cash in Payment for Goods bought at their Sales, when six Months Interest is due upon them.” (Massie 1750, pp. 25–26, quoted at p. 371)

**How to use it.** This is the core of the comparative investment argument. The EIC could borrow at 3–4% on flexible terms, with a deep London market willing to hold and price its paper. That is a concrete, measurable advantage over the VOC, whose shares became illiquid after 1720 and whose dividends were funded by borrowing. Use the 1728 and 1737 coupon reductions as evidence that the EIC’s credit improved over the period. Use the “embedded options” point to show that the EIC’s financial engineering was sophisticated, not primitive.

**Limitations.** The article is about EIC *bonds*, not EIC *equity*. It does not give a share-price series, and it does not directly compare EIC and VOC equity returns. Its claim that the London market was integrated is a correction of Dickson and others; do not overstate it as a claim that London was more advanced than Amsterdam. Rousseau (below) shows that Amsterdam was the earlier financial revolution. The point is that by 1725–50 London had caught up in the specific market for EIC paper.

### 3. Phung, “Dutch East India Company’s Investor Behavior after the First Financial Bubble 1720” (2013)

**Citation.** Phung, Thanh Ngoc Thi. “Dutch East India Company’s Investor Behavior after the First Financial Bubble 1720.” Master’s thesis, Erasmus University Rotterdam, 2013. https://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=131020.

**Type.** Secondary; English; quantitative thesis using hand-collected VOC ledger data.

**What it argues.** After the 1720 bubble, no firms on the Amsterdam exchange issued new shares for more than a decade. Using VOC trading records, Phung finds a structural break in VOC trading volume after 1720, with liquidity at critically low levels for nearly a decade. Investors’ demand and willingness to trade fell sharply. The evidence on fundamental value is inconclusive, but there are signs of loss aversion: investors tended to hold on to losing stock rather than sell.

**Key claims and page references.**
- There was a significant break in VOC trading volume after 1720; liquidity remained critically low for nearly a decade (Abstract, p. 4).
- The volume data cover 1704–1729 and 65 of the most active investors, with 4,171 observations and 298 monthly trading volumes (pp. 16–17).
- The average monthly liquidity in the selected accounts was more than 48,500 guilders (p. 17).
- The VOC never issued stock certificates; ownership was recorded as a balance in the Company’s ledger (p. 17).
- Stock prices after 1720 are available only twice a month, from Van Dillen’s series for 1723–1794 (p. 16).
- The thesis tests whether dividend yields after 1720 were lower than bond yields, and whether investors held losing stocks (pp. 15–16).

**Direct quotations.**
> “There was a significant break in trading volume of VOC after the year 1720 and liquidity for the stock remained at a critically low level for nearly a decade afterwards, implying investors’ significantly lower demand and willingness to trade.” (Abstract, p. 4)

> “The VOC never issued stock certificates so the only evidence of an investor’s share ownership was the balance on the account under his name in the ledger of the VOC.” (p. 17)

**How to use it.** This is your best evidence that the VOC was not a liquid investment in the 1720s and 1730s. It supports the claim that an investor who wanted to buy or sell VOC stock in 1725–50 faced a thin market and high implicit costs. It also supports the “dividend trap” argument: a high dividend yield is less attractive if the capital is illiquid and the fundamental value is uncertain. Use it alongside Taylor’s long-run return figures.

**Limitations.** The thesis is about 1704–1729, not the whole 1725–50 period, and it focuses on a sample of 65 active investors. It does not prove that VOC shares were impossible to sell, only that trading volume fell sharply. It is a master’s thesis, not a peer-reviewed article; use it for the liquidity point, not as the sole basis for a strong quantitative claim. The dividend-yield tests are inconclusive, so do not say the thesis “proves” that VOC dividends were unsustainable — Zheng and Taylor do that work.

### 4. Rousseau, “Historical Perspectives on Financial Development and Economic Growth” (2002)

**Citation.** Rousseau, Peter L. “Historical Perspectives on Financial Development and Economic Growth.” NBER Working Paper 9333, November 2002. https://www.nber.org/papers/w9333.

**Type.** Secondary; English; macroeconomic history.

**What it argues.** Financial development — the emergence of instruments, institutions, and markets — played a central role in promoting trade and growth in the Dutch Republic, England, the United States, and Japan. Amsterdam was the site of the first financial revolution; England’s financial system was in place by 1750 and helped prepare industrialization.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The VOC was chartered in 1602 with 2,167 shares at a par value of 3,000 guilders each; owners could liquidate through the Company once every ten years (p. 3).
- When the directors repudiated that provision at the end of the first decade, a secondary market emerged: shares and futures began to trade on the Amsterdam bourse, “the world’s first modern securities market” (p. 3).
- The VOC held a statutory and practical monopoly on Asiatic trade east of the Cape of Good Hope (p. 3).
- Rousseau dates the Bank of Amsterdam’s start to 1602 and argues that it supported the stock market as well as commodity trade (pp. 3–4). The conventional founding date is 1609; the discrepancy does not affect the argument, but do not repeat Rousseau’s date without a note.
- Neal’s VOC share-price series runs from 1723 to 1794 (p. 4).
- England, with the key components of a financial system in place by 1750, was poised to industrialize (p. 2).

**Direct quotations.**
> “The VOC was capitalized with 2,167 shares at a par value of 3,000 fl. each, and the owners could liquidate their stakes through the Company once every ten years. But when the Directors repudiated this provision at the end of the first decade, those wishing to liquidate needed a secondary market. It was in this climate that shares and futures began to trade on the Amsterdam bourse — the world’s first modern securities market.” (p. 3)

**How to use it.** Rousseau establishes the institutional contrast: the VOC helped create the first modern securities market, but the Dutch advantage was early, not permanent. By the 1720s–1750s, London had the deeper and more flexible market for the EIC’s paper. Use Rousseau for the “Amsterdam was first” point and then pivot to the EIC’s cheaper capital. It also gives you the 2,167 shares / 3,000 guilders detail for the company’s structure.

**Limitations.** Rousseau is a macro-econometric paper, not a comparative history of the two companies. It does not argue that the EIC was a better investment. Its VOC price series starts in 1723, which is convenient for the 1725–50 window but does not cover the earlier bubble. Do not use it to claim that Amsterdam declined in absolute terms; the Dutch market remained sophisticated. The point is relative: London’s market for EIC debt and equity became deeper and cheaper.

### 5. Price and Whatley, “The Transatlantic Slave Trade, the South Sea Company and the Financial Revolution in Great Britain” (2018)

**Citation.** Price, Gregory N., and Warren Whatley. “The Transatlantic Slave Trade, the South Sea Company and the Financial Revolution in Great Britain.” Working paper, December 27, 2018. https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2019/preliminary/paper/i3FRDG5N. Revised version published as “Did Profitable Slave Trading Enable the Expansion of Empire? The Asiento de Negros, the South Sea Company and the Financial Revolution in Great Britain.” *Cliometrica* 15, no. 3 (2021): 675–718. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11698-020-00219-w.

**Type.** Secondary; English; quantitative economic history with an explicit ethical dimension.

**What it argues.** The South Sea Company was central to the British financial revolution. Parliament chartered it in 1711 to convert illiquid short-term public debt into tradable equity, and awarded it the Asiento — the monopoly right to supply enslaved Africans to the Spanish Empire. The authors estimate a CAPM and find that slave trading created substantial positive risk-adjusted returns for South Sea shareholders, especially when the government used the Company to refinance public debt. They argue that slave trading was a critical component of Britain’s ability to commit to repay its debt and to win the imperial race.

**Key claims and page references.**
- Between 1711 and 1725, the cost of long-term British government borrowing fell from 8.7% to approximately 3% (p. 3).
- The South Sea Company held the Asiento from 1713 to 1743 (p. 4).
- In months when at least one South Sea Company slave ship departed London for Africa and the South Seas, the monthly risk-adjusted return on South Sea stock increased by an estimated 7.8% (p. 4).
- The Company timed slave-ship departures to entice the public to convert debt into shares when the government needed refinancing (p. 4).
- The authors connect this to the Williams thesis: slave trading was profitable and helped finance imperial expansion (p. 4).

**Direct quotations.**
> “In months when at least one South Sea Company slave ship departed London for Africa and the South Seas, the monthly risk-adjusted return on South Sea Company stock increased by an estimated 7.8%.” (p. 4)

> “The government also awarded the Company an international monopoly to supply slaves to the Spanish Empire.” (p. 1)

**How to use it.** This is the ethics section’s strongest evidence. It shows that the British financial revolution — the same system that made the EIC’s cheap capital possible — was entangled with slavery and monopoly. Use it to argue that the choice between the VOC and the EIC is not a choice between a clean company and a dirty one. Both were chartered monopolies embedded in coercive colonial systems. The ethical alternative is not “invest in the nicer monopoly”; it is to refuse the structure and to support transparency, abolition, and free trade.

**Limitations.** The paper is about the South Sea Company, not the EIC. Do not imply that the EIC was the Asiento holder or that its returns came directly from slave trading. The connection is systemic: the British financial revolution, of which the EIC was a part, was financed in part by slave-trading profits and monopoly rents. The 7.8% figure is a monthly risk-adjusted return for South Sea stock, not an annual return, and not an EIC figure. Use it carefully and attribute it precisely.

### 6. Taylor, *Five Financial Eras* (2026)

**Citation.** Taylor, Bryan. *Five Financial Eras: How Financial Markets Transformed the World.* Charlottesville, VA: CFA Institute Research Foundation, 2026. https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/foundation/2026/five-financial-eras. PDF: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/docs/research-reports/rf_taylor_fivefinancialeras_online.pdf.

**Type.** Secondary; English; long-run financial-market history with return data.

**What it argues.** The period 1720–1763 was a “Mercantilist Economy” with low stock returns, low bond returns, and low equity risk premia. The bubbles of 1719–1720 scarred investors; interest in equities remained low for the rest of the century. Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands hit new lows at the beginning of 1762. The VOC found it harder to make profits and chose to issue more debt rather than new shares, eventually creating a debt load that forced it into bankruptcy.

**Key claims and page references.**
- 1720–1763 stock market returns: United Kingdom 2.94% per year; Netherlands 1.18% per year (p. 33).
- Bear markets: stocks declined between 1720 and 1762 (p. 33).
- Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands all hit new lows at the beginning of 1762 (p. 34).
- “Shares in French Compagnie des Indes Orientales and Dutch VOC shares gradually declined between 1721 and 1763.” (p. 34)
- The VOC chose to issue more debt rather than new shares, creating a debt load that would force it into bankruptcy (p. 34).
- British government debt rose from £53.9 million in 1720 to £134.2 million in 1763, while stock market capitalization fell (p. 34).
- The 1720–1763 period had few major wars until 1756, relatively light government intervention, and slight deflation (p. 33).

**Direct quotations.**
> “Shares in French Compagnie des Indes Orientales and Dutch VOC shares gradually declined between 1721 and 1763. The VOC found it more difficult to make profits in the 1700s. It chose to issue more debt rather than new shares, eventually creating a debt load that would force it into bankruptcy.” (p. 34)

> “Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands all hit new lows at the beginning of 1762. There were no major bull markets for four decades as most investors chose safe government bonds over risky equities.” (p. 34)

> “Stock Market Returns: United Kingdom: 2.94%; Netherlands: 1.18%.” (p. 33)

**How to use it.** Taylor is the essential counterweight to the old paper’s “cash out in 1748–50 at the post-war peak” claim. There was no post-war peak. The whole period from 1721 to 1763 was a secular bear market for both companies. The best you can say is that the late 1740s were a **least-bad exit window** — a moment of relative liquidity and temporary war relief — not a top. Taylor also supports the “VOC issued debt, not equity” point, which explains why its shares were illiquid.

**Limitations.** Taylor is a synthesis of long-run index data, not a company-level study. The 2.94% and 1.18% figures are market-level returns, not the returns of a specific investor in EIC or VOC shares. The book is published by the CFA Institute Research Foundation, which is a professional body, not a peer-reviewed journal. Use it for the big picture and pair it with Nogués-Marco and Phung for the company-level detail.

### 7. De la Vega, *Confusión de Confusiones* (1688) — Spanish

**Citation.** De la Vega, Joseph. *Confusión de Confusiones: Diálogos Curiosos entre un Filósofo Agudo, un Mercader Discreto, y un Accionista Erudito.* Amsterdam, 1688. Koninklijke Bibliotheek copy, Internet Archive. https://archive.org/details/ned-kbn-all-00002508-001.

**Type.** Primary; Spanish; printed in Amsterdam in 1688. This is the classic contemporary account of the Amsterdam bourse.

**What it is.** A dialogue in three parts between a philosopher, a merchant, and a learned shareholder (the *Accionista*). It describes the business of shares — their origin, etymology, reality, game, and entanglement — and captures the speculative culture of the Amsterdam market. It is not a neutral treatise; it is a literary and moralizing account, but it is eyewitness evidence of how the market worked.

**Key claims and details.**
- The title page describes the book as a dialogue “descubriendo el negocio de las Acciones, su origen, su ethimología, su realidad, su juego, y su enredo” (discovering the business of shares, their origin, etymology, reality, game, and entanglement). The original spelling is “ethimología.”
- The book was printed in Amsterdam in 1688 and is in Spanish.
- It is the source Rousseau cites for the claim that shares and futures began to trade on the Amsterdam bourse (Rousseau, p. 3).
- It describes the *Accionista* as a distinct social type — the shareholder as a market actor with his own knowledge and psychology.
- It captures the importance of rumor, opinion, and timing in share trading, not just fundamental value.

**Direct quotation (Spanish, title page).**
> “Confusión de Confusiones. Diálogos Curiosos entre un Filósofo Agudo, un Mercader Discreto, y un Accionista Erudito. Descubriendo el negocio de las Acciones, su origen, su ethimología, su realidad, su juego, y su enredo.” (title page, 1688; original spelling “ethimología”)

**Translation.**
> “Confusion of Confusions. Curious Dialogues between a Sharp Philosopher, a Discreet Merchant, and a Learned Shareholder. Discovering the business of shares, their origin, their etymology, their reality, their game, and their entanglement.”

**How to use it.** Use De la Vega to establish that the VOC was trading in a genuinely modern securities market by the 1680s, with speculation, options, and a culture of share trading. This strengthens the contrast with the EIC only if you then show that the VOC’s market advantage eroded. It is also useful for the paper’s opening: the first modern stock market was built around the VOC, which makes the VOC’s later decline more striking. Quote the title page, not the OCR (the OCR is old Spanish and error-prone).

**Limitations.** The book is from 1688, not 1725–50. It is primary evidence for the *early* market, not for the period under study. Use it as context, and do not cite it for 1725–50 prices or dividends. It is also literary and moralizing, not statistical. The Spanish original is available; the English translation by Hermann Kellenbenz (1957) is not needed if you quote the Spanish and translate it yourself.

### 8. *Octrooi van de VOC* (1602) — Dutch

**Citation.** States General of the Netherlands. *Octrooi van de VOC* [Charter of the Dutch East India Company]. March 20, 1602. Nationaal Archief, The Hague, 1.04.02, inv. 1. https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/octrooi-voor-de-voc-1602. Dutch transcription: https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC.

**Type.** Primary; Dutch; the charter itself, printed in 1602. This is the constitutional foundation of the VOC.

**What it is.** The charter granted the VOC a 21-year monopoly on Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and through the Strait of Magellan. It created the Company as a chartered body with sovereign-like powers: the right to make treaties, build forts, appoint governors and judges, raise troops, wage war, and administer justice. The Nationaal Archief’s educational page describes the Company as “een verlengstuk van de Staten-Generaal” and “in feite als een soevereine (onafhankelijke) staat in Azië,” and notes that the charter provided the legal basis for slavery and for the 1621 genocide on the Banda islands. Use the introduction for context, but cite the charter for the legal claims.

**Key claims and details.**
- Article XXXIV: no one other than the Company may sail east of the Cape of Good Hope or through the Strait of Magellan for 21 years, on pain of forfeiture of ships and goods.
- Article XXXV: the Company may make treaties with Asian princes and potentates in the name of the States General, build forts, appoint governors, soldiers, and officers of justice, and wage war.
- The charter creates a monopoly, not a free market.
- The charter is the legal basis for the VOC’s sovereign-like conduct in Asia, including slavery and conquest.
- The Nationaal Archief notes that the Company could take people captured in a “just war” as slaves, and that the charter underpinned the conquest and colonization of Jakarta (1619), the Banda genocide (1621), and the occupation of Taiwan (1622–63).

**Direct quotation (original Dutch, art. XXXIV).**
> “Ende op dat het voornemen van deze Compagnie met meerder vrugt mag uitgevoert worden … zoo hebben wy de voorsz Compagnie geoctroyeert ende geaccordeert, octroyeren ende accorderen mits dezen, dat niemant, van wat qualiteit ofte conditie die zy, anders dan die van de voorsz Compagnie uit deze vereenigde Landen zal mogen vaaren, binnen den tyd van 21 jaaren eerstkomende, beginnende met dezen jaare 1602. incluis, Beoosten de kaap de Bonne Esperance, ofte door de straat van Magellanes, op de verbeurte van de schepen en goederen…” (art. XXXIV)

**Translation (Nationaal Archief’s modern Dutch rendering).**
> “…dat niemand van welke aard of kwaliteit anders dan die van de voorschreven Compagnie uit deze Verenigde provincies zal mogen varen binnen den tijd van een en twintig jaren eerstkomend, te beginnen met dit jaar 1602 ten oosten van de Kaap de Goede Hoop of door de Straat van Magellanes op straffe van verbeurte van de schepen en goederen.” (Nationaal Archief)

**English translation.**
> “…that no one of whatever nature or quality, other than those of the aforesaid Company from these United Provinces, may sail within the time of twenty-one years next coming, beginning this year 1602, east of the Cape of Good Hope or through the Strait of Magellan, on pain of forfeiture of the ships and goods.”

**Direct quotation (art. XXXV, sovereign powers).**
> “Item, dat die van de voorsz Compagnie zullen vermogen Beoosten de kaap de Bonne Esperance … met de Princen ende Potentaten verbintenis te maaken, ende contracten op den naam van de Staaten Generaal … mitsgaders aldaar eenige fortressen ende verzekertheeden te bouwen, gouverneurs, volk van oorlog, ende officiers van Justitie … te stellen…” (art. XXXV)

**Translation.** “Item, that those of the aforesaid Company may, east of the Cape of Good Hope … make alliances and contracts with princes and potentates in the name of the States General … and build forts and strongholds there, appoint governors, soldiers, and officers of justice…”

**Direct quotation (Nationaal Archief, ethics).**
> “De VOC mag van de Staten-Generaal bovendien gebieden met geweld innemen en deze vervolgens besturen en exploiteren. … De VOC opereert in feite als soevereine (onafhankelijke) staat in Azië.” (Nationaal Archief)

> “Daarmee staat de Staten-Generaal toe dat de VOC mensen die in een ‘rechtvaardige oorlog’ gevangen worden genomen tot slaaf maakt.” (Nationaal Archief)

**Translation.** “The VOC was moreover permitted by the States General to seize territories by force and then govern and exploit them. … The VOC operated in effect as a sovereign (independent) state in Asia.” / “The States General thus permitted the VOC to enslave people captured in a ‘just war.’”

**How to use it.** This is the primary source for the claim that the VOC was a chartered monopoly with sovereign powers. Use it to set up the comparison with the EIC: both were monopolies created by charter, but the VOC’s charter was older, broader, and more state-like. Use the Nationaal Archief’s introduction for the ethical point that the Company’s powers included coercion, slavery, and violence. Do not cite the introduction as if it were the charter; distinguish the primary text from the modern commentary.

**Limitations.** The charter is from 1602, not 1725–50. It establishes the original legal structure, not the Company’s finances in the later period. The Dutch is early modern and the Wikisource transcription may contain errors; quote only the clause you can verify. The Nationaal Archief page is a teaching resource, not a scholarly edition. For a stronger citation, use the inventory reference (1.04.02, inv. 1) and the archive’s scan.

### 9. Van Imhoff, *Considérations sur l’état présent de la Compagnie Hollandoise des Indes Orientales* (1741/1763) — French

**Citation.** Van Imhoff, Gustaaf Willem. *Considérations sur l’état présent de la Compagnie Hollandoise des Indes Orientales, relativement à sa navigation, à son commerce & à son gouvernement, & sur les moyens de remédier aux causes de sa décadence.* 1741. French edition appended to J. P. J. Du Bois, *Vies des gouverneurs généraux, avec l’abrégé de l’histoire des établissemens hollandois aux Indes Orientales*. The Hague: Pierre de Hondt, 1763. https://archive.org/details/viesdesgouverne00imhogoog.

**Type.** Primary; French (translated from Dutch); the memorandum was written in 1741 by the VOC’s Governor-General, and the French edition was printed in 1763. This is the most important primary source for the VOC’s internal diagnosis of decline.

**What it is.** A memorandum by Gustaaf Willem van Imhoff, who served as Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies (1743–1750). He identifies three main causes of the Company’s decline and proposes remedies. The French edition is appended to a history of the Dutch governors-general; the *Considérations* occupy about 48 pages at the end.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The Company is “not today in the flourishing state it once was” (pp. 3–4).
- Three main causes: (1) the too-great extent of its possessions in the Indies, which makes administration harder and more prone to problems; (2) the excessive abundance of Eastern produce and the resulting fall in its price in Europe; (3) the relaxation of zeal and attention among the Company’s employees, due to lack of esteem for rank, reward for merit, and punishment proportional to offenses (pp. 3–4).
- The second cause is attributed to all maritime nations of Europe, who after the Peace of Utrecht applied themselves to navigation and commerce; freight, interest, and maritime risk leave almost no profit on most articles (p. 4).
- The Company has too many ships, too many men, and too much expense; he proposes smaller crews and different ship designs (pp. 5–7).
- He criticizes the practice of employing 120 men on the largest return ships and recommends 50–60, as other nations do (p. 5).
- He calls for better training, discipline, and rewards for officers (pp. 5–7).
- The larger Du Bois volume in which the French edition is bound includes a plate list entry for the “Soulevement & Massacre des Chinois” at Batavia (p. 315); the *Considérations* itself focuses on navigation and commerce, but the volume is a reminder of the violence that underpinned the Company’s rule.

**Direct quotations (French).**
> “Sans contredit la Compagnie Hollandoise des Indes Orientales n’est pas aujourd’hui dans cet état florissant où elle étoit autrefois. On en allègue différentes causes directes & indirectes, que l’on peut réduire à trois principales: 1°. la trop grande étendue de ses possessions dans les Indes, ce qui en rend l’administration moins aisée & plus sujette à des inconvéniens. 2°. L’abondance excessive des productions de l’Orient, & par conséquent la diminution de leur prix en Europe: enfin 3°. le relâchement de zèle & d’attention de la part des Employés au Service de la Compagnie…” (pp. 3–4; modernized from the OCR, which prints long s as f)

> “Il n’y a presque aucun bénéfice sur la plupart des articles, dont le trafic est devenu commun.” (p. 4)

**Translation.**
> “The Dutch East India Company is undoubtedly not today in the flourishing state it once was. Various direct and indirect causes are alleged, which can be reduced to three main ones: (1) the too-great extent of its possessions in the Indies, which makes administration less easy and more prone to problems; (2) the excessive abundance of Eastern produce, and consequently the fall in its price in Europe; finally (3) the relaxation of zeal and attention on the part of the employees in the Company’s service…”

> “There is almost no profit on most articles, whose trade has become common.”

**How to use it.** Van Imhoff is the primary-source anchor for the “VOC was already declining before 1750” argument. He was not a hostile outsider; he was the Company’s own Governor-General. Use the three causes to structure the VOC section: over-extension, falling prices, and employee corruption. The shipping-cost passage is useful for the “high fixed costs” point. The Batavia reference is useful for the ethics section, but handle it carefully: the book’s title page mentions the massacre, but the *Considérations* itself is mainly about navigation and commerce. Do not imply that Van Imhoff condemns the massacre; he does not, in the passages I have read.

**Limitations.** The French edition is a translation of a 1741 Dutch memorandum, printed in 1763. If your instructor insists on a source printed in 1725–50, note that the original is in-period but the print is later. The OCR is old French and contains errors; check any quotation against the page image. The edition is appended to a larger book, so the page numbers are the book’s page numbers, not a standalone pamphlet. If you need the Dutch original, the scholarly edition is in *Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde van Nederlandsch-Indië* 66 (1912), pp. 483–513, but that is a modern edition.

### 10. Van Dam, *Beschryvinge van de Oostindische Compagnie* (1693–1701) — Dutch

**Citation.** Van Dam, Pieter. *Beschryvinge van de Oostindische Compagnie.* 1693–1701. Digital edition, Huygens Institute for the History of the Netherlands. https://resources.huygens.knaw.nl/retroboeken/vandam/.

**Type.** Primary text; Dutch; written in 1693–1701 by the VOC’s secretary/advocate. The digital edition is modern and scholarly, not a period print.

**What it is.** A detailed description of the VOC’s organization, governance, trade, and operations, written from inside the Company. It is one of the richest sources for how the VOC actually worked: its chambers, directors, bookkeeping, shipping, and legal privileges. It is not a polemic; it is an institutional description, which makes it useful for explaining the Company’s structure.

**Key claims and details (scope, not quotation).**
- The VOC was a federation of six chambers (Amsterdam, Zeeland, Rotterdam, Delft, Hoorn, and Enkhuizen), governed by the Heren XVII.
- The Company combined trade with sovereign-like functions: forts, troops, treaties, and justice.
- The Amsterdam chamber was the largest and most important.
- The Company’s bookkeeping and governance were complex, which made it hard for outsiders to assess its finances.

**How to use it.** Use Van Dam for the institutional description of the VOC, especially the six chambers and the Heren XVII. It helps explain why the VOC was a state-like body, not just a firm. Use it as context, not as a source of quantitative claims. If you need a direct quotation, check the Dutch text in the Huygens edition and translate it yourself; do not rely on a secondary paraphrase.

**Limitations.** The work was written in 1693–1701 but not printed until the twentieth century. The Huygens edition is a modern scholarly edition, so it may not satisfy a strict “period primary” requirement. It is also in Dutch and the digital edition is a web application; extracting a precise page citation requires care. For the paper, it is safer to use it as background and to put the weight of the primary argument on Van Imhoff, the Octrooi, and the English pamphlets.

### 11. *A Letter to the Chairman of the East-India Company* (1727) — English

**Citation.** [Proprietor in the Company’s Stock]. *A Letter to the Chairman of the East-India Company: In Which the Facts Contained in the Memorials to the Court of Directors, and the Proceedings of the Last General Court, upon the Answer of the Court of Directors to Those Memorials, Are Fully Stated and Shown.* London, 1727. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-letter-to-the-chairman_proprietor-in-the-compan_1727.

**Type.** Primary; English; a period pamphlet by an EIC shareholder. It is polemical, but it is direct evidence of investor discourse in the 1720s.

**What it is.** A defence of the EIC’s directors against a group of memorialists who accused them of self-dealing in the Company’s sales. The author, a proprietor, argues that the charges are exaggerated, that the directors acted in the Company’s interest, and that the critics are disappointed bidders. It is useful for showing how shareholders talked about governance, monopoly, and profit.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The author says the EIC is “an exceeding beneficial Branch of our Commerce” and that “this is a beneficial Trade” (p. 5).
- He defends the directors against charges that they used their knowledge of the September sale to buy goods cheaply: “by this means they made great advantage of their Knowledge, by the Monopoly of those Commodities” (p. 22). The context is an accusation, not a confession; the author is paraphrasing the critics’ charge.
- He insists that the only relevant question for a proprietor is whether the Company lost money: “what is the only point to the Proprietors, any Loss to the Company, by the misconduct of the Directors?” (p. 25).
- He argues that encouraging many buyers “prevents all Practice and Combinations, destroys Monopolies” (p. 27). This is a defense of open bidding within the Company’s sale, not a defense of free trade in general.
- The pamphlet shows that EIC shareholders were actively debating governance and directors’ conduct in the 1720s.

**Direct quotations.**
> “And that this is a beneficial Trade, is evident to any Man acquainted with the Imports and Exports of the Company.” (p. 5)

> “By this means they made great advantage of their Knowledge, by the Monopoly of those Commodities.” (p. 22)

> “What is the only point to the Proprietors, any Loss to the Company, by the misconduct of the Directors?” (p. 25; modernized from the OCR, which prints “L,8/5” for “Loss”)

> “This prevents all Practice and Combinations, destroys Monopolies…” (p. 27; modernized from “deſtroys”)

**How to use it.** Use the 1727 pamphlet to show that EIC shareholders had a voice, a public sphere, and a concept of accountability. The directors were criticized in print, and the pamphlet responds in print. That is a contrast with the VOC, where Zheng says directors withheld financial information from shareholders. The “only point to the Proprietors” quote is useful for the investment decision: the shareholder’s bottom line is profit, not imperial glory. The “destroys Monopolies” quote is tricky; it refers to competition among buyers at the Company’s sale, not to the Company’s own monopoly. Do not quote it as an anti-monopoly statement without explaining the context.

**Limitations.** The pamphlet is a defence of the directors, so it is biased. It does not give a balanced view of the charges. It is about a specific sales scandal in 1727, not about the Company’s long-run finances. Use it for investor discourse and governance, not for financial data. The OCR is readable but old; check quotations against the page images.

### 12. *A Collection of Papers Relating to the East India Trade* (1730) — English

**Citation.** *A Collection of Papers Relating to the East India Trade: Wherein Are Shewn the Disadvantages to a Nation, by Confining Any Trade to a Corporation with a Joint-Stock.* London: J. Walthoe, 1730. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-collection-of-papers-r_1730.

**Type.** Primary; English; a period pamphlet collection, printed in 1730. It reprints earlier papers (1700 and 1717) and adds an introduction.

**What it is.** A free-trade critique of the EIC’s joint-stock monopoly. The introduction argues that confining trade to a corporation with a joint stock is, “to all intents and purposes, a Monopoly,” even if Parliament has sanctioned it. The collection is useful for the ethical and policy alternative: private trade, open competition, and limits on chartered privilege.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The subtitle states the thesis: “the Disadvantages to a Nation, by confining any Trade to a Corporation with a Joint-Stock” (title page).
- The introduction argues that a corporation with a parliamentary sanction and an exclusive trade is still a monopoly “to all Intents and Purposes” (p. vi).
- It reprints papers from 1700 and 1717, which shows that the critique of the EIC monopoly was long-standing, not a one-off (p. vi).
- It argues that exclusive companies raise prices and restrict trade, and that private merchants are more efficient (pp. vi–vii).

**Direct quotations.**
> “…confining any Trade to a Corporation with a Joint-Stock.” (title page)

> “That however any Corporation may have the Sanction of Parliament for the carrying on a Trade, exclusive of all other their fellow Subject, yet that notwithstanding, they are to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly…” (p. vi; modernized from the OCR: “Santtion of Farliament,” “excluſrve 4,” “notwithftanaing”)

**How to use it.** This is the best primary source for the “ethical alternative” section. It gives you a period argument that the EIC’s charter was a monopoly even though it was legal, and that private trade was a better alternative. Use it to argue that the choice between the VOC and the EIC was not a choice between monopoly and competition; both were monopolies. The ethical alternative was to open the trade, not to pick the less bad monopolist. It also supports the paper’s claim that the EIC’s advantage was institutional, not moral.

**Limitations.** The pamphlet is a polemic by free-trade interests, not a neutral analysis. It reprints older papers and may exaggerate the benefits of private trade. It is about the EIC, not the VOC, so use it for the EIC side and for the general critique of chartered monopoly. The OCR is old but readable; check the page numbers against the images.

### 13. *Three Letters to the Proprietors of Stock in the East-India Company* (1749) — English

**Citation.** [A Director]. *Three Letters to the Proprietors of Stock in the East-India Company, Relative to the Question to Be Ballotted for on Wednesday, January 3d, 1749.* London: M. Cooper, 1749. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_three-letters-to-the-pro_director_1749.

**Type.** Primary; English; a period pamphlet by a dissenting EIC director, printed in 1749. This is the closest source in the set to the cash-out window.

**What it is.** A series of letters to EIC stockholders about a ballot on the Company’s acceptance of the government’s proposal to reduce the interest on the national debt. The proposal would have reduced the interest the Company received on £3.2 million of government debt from 4% to 3.5% (from December 1750) and then to 3% (from December 1757). The author, a director, argues that the Company should not accept the proposal on those terms, and that the proprietors should postpone the decision.

**Key claims and page references.**
- The ballot was to be held at East India House on Wednesday, January 3, 1749 (title page).
- The question was whether the Company should accept 4% until December 25, 1750, then 3.5% until December 25, 1757, and then 3% until redeemed by Parliament, on £3.2 million of government debt (pp. 1–2).
- The Company’s capital stock was £3.2 million: “the Debt on the Two First Loans amounting to 3,200,000 l. which is the Company’s Capital Stock” (p. 22).
- The author argues that the Company should be allowed to issue new bonds or annuities on better terms, and that the bondholders should have the preference (pp. 22–24).
- The pamphlet shows that EIC proprietors voted on questions that directly affected the value of their stock, and that directors could dissent publicly (pp. 22–26).

**Direct quotations.**
> “Three Letters to the Proprietors of Stock in the East-India Company, Relative to the Question to Be Ballotted for on Wednesday, January 3d, 1749.” (title page)

> “…the Debt on the Two First Loans amounting to 3,200,000 l. which is the Company’s Capital Stock…” (p. 22)

**How to use it.** This is your primary anchor for the cash-out section. It shows that in January 1749 — right in the middle of the 1745–50 window — EIC proprietors were actively voting on the Company’s capital structure. It does not give a share price, but it gives you the decision context: the Company was managing its debt, the proprietors had a ballot, and a director was publicly arguing for better terms. Use it to make the cash-out decision concrete: an investor in 1749 was not passively watching a price chart; he was participating in a financial institution with governance, debt management, and contested votes.

**Limitations.** The pamphlet is about the interest reduction on government debt, not about the decision to sell EIC shares. Do not claim that it proves a share-price peak or a cash-out recommendation. It is evidence of investor politics, not of market timing. The author is a director with a particular view; he is not a neutral observer. The printed page numbers are in parentheses; the title page is unnumbered.

### 14. Savary des Bruslons, *Dictionnaire universel de commerce* (1742) — French (optional)

**Citation.** Savary des Bruslons, Jacques. *Dictionnaire universel de commerce, contenant tout ce qui concerne le commerce qui se fait dans les quatre parties du monde.* Paris, 1742. Vol. 1. https://archive.org/details/b30450470_0001.

**Type.** Primary; French; a period commercial reference work, printed in 1742. It is useful for the non-English requirement and for period definitions of shares, companies, and commerce.

**What it is.** A commercial dictionary with entries on companies, shares (*actions*), and trade. The entry on *actions* discusses the shares of trading companies, including the French and Dutch companies, and explains how share prices rise and fall with the company’s credit and profits.

**Key claims and details.**
- The dictionary has an entry on *actions* (shares) that discusses the shares of commercial companies (vol. 1, entry “Actions”).
- It explains that the shares of trading companies rise and fall with the company’s credit and the public’s confidence (vol. 1, entry “Actions”).
- It provides period vocabulary for talking about shareholders, capital, and company stock.

**How to use it.** Use Savary for period definitions and for a French perspective on company shares. It is optional; the paper already has four non-English sources without it. If you need a fifth non-English source or a period definition of “actions,” use it. Do not rely on it for quantitative claims; the OCR is old French and the entries are long.

**Limitations.** It is a reference work, not a narrative source. The OCR is difficult, and the page numbers are not easy to pin down. It is not directly about the VOC or the EIC. Use it only if you need a period definition or an additional French source.

## C. What I verified, and what I did not

**Verified.**
- Every link in this file returns the document described, not a homepage.
- The page numbers and quotations above were checked against the extracted text or the page images.
- The key figures: VOC employees (7,700 in 1625; ~35,000 in 1750), salaries (8,887,198 guilders in 1705–07; 12,289,055 in 1766), average dividend (18%), EIC India bond coupons (4% in 1728; 3% in 1737), EIC capital stock (£3.2 million in 1749), stock returns 1720–1763 (UK 2.94%; Netherlands 1.18%), and the 1762 lows.
- The old paper’s “cash out in 1748–50 at the post-war peak” claim is **not** supported by these sources. Taylor shows a secular decline from 1721 to 1763, with new lows in 1762. The correct formulation is a “least-bad exit window” in the late 1740s, not a peak.

**Not verified / gaps.**
- I did not find a free, digitized run of *The Course of the Exchange* for 1725–50. Nogués-Marco uses it, and Taylor cites it, but the original price current is behind library databases. If you want a period share-price series, the next step is to check HathiTrust, ECCO, or a university library.
- I did not read the Dutch original of Van Imhoff’s memorandum (1741); I used the 1763 French translation. If your instructor requires a period Dutch print, the original may be harder to find; the 1912 *Bijdragen* edition is a modern scholarly edition.
- I did not extract a direct quotation from Van Dam’s Dutch text; I used it for institutional context. If you want a Van Dam quote, check the Huygens edition and translate it yourself.
- I did not verify the exact EIC share price in 1748 or 1749. The 1749 pamphlet gives the decision context, not the price. Do not invent a number.

**How to use the notes in the paper.**
- For the VOC decline: Zheng (costs, corruption, dividend trap) + Van Imhoff (three causes) + Taylor (long-run returns).
