# Refutation and Second-Draft Pass

**Purpose.** This is the assignment’s “refutation” step. It does three things: (1) it audits the claims in the earlier draft, (2) it states the strongest objections to the new thesis and answers them, and (3) it lists the revisions that the second draft must make. Do not skip this step; it is the difference between a paper that asserts and a paper that argues.

---

## Part 1: Audit of the earlier draft

### Claim 1: “Cash out in 1748–50, at the post-war peak.”

**Verdict: refuted.** The claim assumes a peak that the evidence does not support.

**Evidence:**
- Taylor, *Five Financial Eras*, p. 34: “Shares in French Compagnie des Indes Orientales and Dutch VOC shares gradually declined between 1721 and 1763.” The same page says stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands “all hit new lows at the beginning of 1762.”
- Taylor, p. 33: 1720–1763 stock returns were 2.94% a year in the United Kingdom and 1.18% a year in the Netherlands. That is a secular bear market, not a cycle with a clear post-war peak.
- The 1749 pamphlet shows a proprietors’ ballot on the interest reduction, not a share-price peak. It is evidence of a decision window, not a top.

**Revision:** Replace “peak” with “least-bad exit window.” Explain that 1748–49 was a moment of relative liquidity and political relief after the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle, not a price maximum. The recommendation becomes: exit into the post-war liquidity, before the Seven Years’ War and the 1762 lows. This is defensible even if the exact price is unknown.

**What would restore the original claim:** a primary share-price series showing a local maximum in 1748–50. I could not find a free digitized run of *The Course of the Exchange* for those years. If you find one, you can test the claim; until then, do not assert it.

---

### Claim 2: “The VOC was a strong dividend payer, so it was a good investment.”

**Verdict: misleading.** The dividend was real, but it was financed by borrowing and did not translate into a high total return.

**Evidence:**
- Zheng, p. 113: “Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist.”
- Zheng, p. 113: by 1780 the Company had a loan of nearly 19 million guilders.
- Phung, Abstract, p. 4: VOC trading volume broke after 1720, and liquidity remained “critically low” for nearly a decade.
- Taylor, p. 34: the VOC chose to issue more debt rather than new shares, “eventually creating a debt load that would force it into bankruptcy.”

**Revision:** Treat the 18% dividend as a *financing strategy*, not a sign of profitability. The paper should say: “The VOC’s headline yield was high, but it was funded by debt and accompanied by collapsing liquidity and falling long-run returns. A high dividend is not the same as a high total return.” This is a more sophisticated claim and it is supported by the sources.

**What would restore the original claim:** a total-return series showing that VOC shareholders earned more than EIC shareholders after reinvesting dividends. Taylor’s market-level figures point the other way, but a company-level series would be needed to settle it. Do not assert the opposite without that series either.

---

### Claim 3: “The EIC was the more modern, progressive company.”

**Verdict: partly true, but misleading if stated as a moral claim.** The EIC was more modern in its capital market, not in its ethics.

**Evidence:**
- Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371: the EIC’s India bonds were sophisticated instruments with embedded options; the coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737.
- 1727 pamphlet, p. 25: EIC shareholders had a public voice and a concept of accountability.
- 1730 pamphlet, p. vi: the EIC was still “to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly.”
- Price and Whatley, p. 4: the British financial revolution was entangled with the South Sea Company’s slave trade.

**Revision:** Separate “financial modernity” from “ethical superiority.” The paper’s claim is that the EIC had a cheaper, more flexible capital market. It is not that the EIC was a better institution morally. Add a sentence: “The EIC’s advantage was institutional and financial, not moral; it remained a chartered monopoly embedded in a coercive empire.”

**What would restore the original claim:** evidence that the EIC’s governance was more accountable in practice, not just in print. The 1727 pamphlet is a defence by a proprietor, not a neutral audit. The 1730 pamphlet is a hostile critique. The evidence is mixed.

---

### Claim 4: “Amsterdam was the leading financial market, so the VOC had the advantage.”

**Verdict: true for the seventeenth century, false for 1725–50.** The Dutch advantage was early, not permanent.

**Evidence:**
- Rousseau, p. 3: the VOC helped create “the world’s first modern securities market” in Amsterdam.
- Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 367: the London domestic market was integrated and capable of pricing sophisticated options by the 1720s–1750s.
- Phung, Abstract, p. 4: the VOC’s own market thinned after 1720.

**Revision:** Use Rousseau to establish the Dutch head start, then show that London caught up in the specific market for EIC paper. The paper should say: “Amsterdam was first; London was deeper by the 1720s.” That is a more precise and defensible claim.

**What would restore the original claim:** evidence that the Amsterdam market remained deeper and cheaper for VOC paper through 1725–50. Phung’s liquidity findings point the other way. Do not assert Dutch superiority without a comparison of market depth.

---

### Claim 5: “The VOC declined because of corruption.”

**Verdict: incomplete.** Corruption was real, but it was one of three structural causes.

**Evidence:**
- Van Imhoff, pp. 3–4: the three causes are over-extended possessions, falling Eastern goods prices in Europe, and the relaxation of zeal and attention among employees.
- Zheng, p. 110: salary costs rose from 8,887,198 guilders in 1705–07 to 12,289,055 in 1766; the Company had about 35,000 employees by 1750.
- Zheng, p. 112: the trade shifted from spices and pepper to cotton and tea, and the VOC was slow to adapt.

**Revision:** Make corruption one part of a structural argument. The paper should say: “Corruption raised costs, but the deeper problem was a cost structure built for a spice monopoly that was disappearing.” That connects Van Imhoff’s insider diagnosis to Zheng’s cost data and Taylor’s long-run returns.

**What would restore the original claim:** evidence that corruption was the dominant cause, not one of several. Van Imhoff’s own list puts over-extension and price shifts first and third; corruption is second. The paper should follow the primary source’s emphasis.

## Part 2: Objections to the new thesis, and responses

### Objection 1: “The VOC paid 18%, so it was obviously the better investment.”

**Response:** The 18% figure is an average dividend, not a total return. It was funded by borrowing, and the shares were illiquid. A high dividend can be a signal of distress: the Company is paying out capital because it has no profitable reinvestment opportunities. The relevant comparison is risk-adjusted total return, and Taylor’s market-level data show Dutch returns of 1.18% a year in 1720–1763, versus 2.94% in the UK. The dividend was compensation for risk, not a free lunch.

**Concession:** If an investor held VOC shares for the dividend and never needed to sell, the high yield might have been attractive. But the paper is about an investor choosing in 1725 and exiting in 1745–50; liquidity and capital risk matter.

---

### Objection 2: “The EIC was just as monopolistic and violent as the VOC. Why recommend it?”

**Response:** The paper does not recommend the EIC as an ethical investment. It recommends it as the better *financial* bet of two bad options. The ethical section argues that the choice itself is compromised, and that the alternative is to refuse the structure. This is consistent: a comparative financial judgment can coexist with a structural ethical critique.

**Concession:** The EIC’s involvement in coercion and its entanglement with the slave-trading financial system are real. The paper should not minimize them. The phrase “better of two bad options” does a lot of work; use it carefully.

---

### Objection 3: “The EIC’s shares were also volatile. Why call it safer?”

**Response:** The paper should not call the EIC “safe.” It should say “less bad” and “better capitalized.” The EIC had cheaper debt, a deeper market, and a more diversified trade portfolio, but it was still exposed to war, political risk, and the secular bear market. The recommendation is relative and time-bound: buy the EIC in 1725, exit in the late 1740s. It is not a perpetual hold.

**Concession:** If the investor’s time horizon was the whole 1720–1763 period, both companies lost ground. The EIC lost less. That is the claim.

---

### Objection 4: “If there was no peak, why exit in the late 1740s rather than earlier or later?”

**Response:** The late 1740s offer a combination of relative liquidity and political relief: the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) ended the War of the Austrian Succession, and the 1749 proprietors’ ballot shows an active capital market. Exiting earlier, in the 1730s, would have avoided some decline but might have been harder in a thin market. Exiting later, in the 1750s, would have meant holding through the Seven Years’ War and the 1762 lows. The late 1740s are the least-bad window, not the perfect one.

**Concession:** Without a price series, the exact month is unknowable. The paper should recommend a window, not a date.

---

### Objection 5: “The 1749 pamphlet is about government debt, not about selling shares. You are overreading it.”

**Response:** The pamphlet is not evidence of a share price or a sell recommendation. It is evidence of the decision environment: EIC proprietors were voting on the Company’s capital structure in January 1749, and a director was publicly arguing for better terms. That supports the claim that 1749 was a moment of active investor politics, not that it was a peak. The paper should use it for context, not as proof of timing.

**Concession:** If the paper needs direct evidence of a sell decision, it does not have it. The recommendation is an inference from liquidity and risk, not a documented trade.

---

### Objection 6: “Taylor’s figures are market-level, not company-level. You are using them to make a company-level claim.”

**Response:** Taylor is used for the macro context: the 1720–1763 period was a secular bear market, and the VOC’s shares declined. The company-level claims are supported by Zheng (VOC costs and dividends), Phung (liquidity), and Nogués-Marco (EIC debt). The paper should attribute each claim to the right source and avoid saying “Taylor proves the VOC’s share price fell every year.”

**Concession:** A company-level total-return series for both companies would be stronger. I could not find a free one. The paper should acknowledge the limitation in a footnote or a sentence.

## Part 3: What would change the conclusion

A good paper says what evidence would falsify its thesis. Include one or two of these in the second draft:

1. **A primary price series showing a 1748–50 peak.** If *The Course of the Exchange* shows EIC or VOC shares at a local maximum in 1748–50, the “least-bad window” claim would need to be revised to “peak.” I could not access a free run.
2. **Evidence that the VOC’s dividends were funded by current profits, not borrowing.** If the Company’s accounts show that the 18% dividend was covered by earnings, the “dividend trap” argument weakens. Zheng says the opposite, but a primary account book would be stronger.
3. **Evidence that the EIC’s cost of capital was higher than the VOC’s.** If the EIC’s effective borrowing cost was above the VOC’s, the comparative investment case would weaken. Nogués-Marco’s coupon data point the other way, but they are about bonds, not equity.
4. **Evidence that the VOC reformed successfully after 1741.** If Van Imhoff’s reforms had restored profitability, the decline narrative would be too deterministic. The later bankruptcy suggests they did not, but the paper should not ignore the possibility.
5. **A different ethical framework.** If the assignment expects a period ethical framework (e.g., just-war theory, Christian conscience, or civic republicanism), the modern “refuse the structure” argument may need to be translated into period terms. The 1730 pamphlet’s free-trade critique is the closest period analogue.

## Part 4: Second-draft revision checklist

**Structure**
- [ ] The introduction states the thesis in one sentence and previews the four moves.
- [ ] Each section answers one of the assignment’s questions.
- [ ] The cash-out section explicitly refutes the “peak” claim.
- [ ] The ethics section gives a concrete alternative, not just a complaint.
- [ ] The conclusion does not introduce new evidence.

**Evidence**
- [ ] Every quotation has a page number.
- [ ] Every page number has been checked against the page image.
- [ ] The 18% dividend is described as an average, not a fixed annual rate.
- [ ] The 4% and 3% coupons are described as coupons, not yields.
- [ ] The 7.8% figure is described as a monthly risk-adjusted return for South Sea stock, not an EIC return.
- [ ] The 1749 pamphlet is described as a ballot on government debt, not a sell recommendation.
- [ ] Taylor’s figures are described as market-level, not company-level.

**Style**
- [ ] No sentence begins with “It is important to note that.”
- [ ] No paragraph ends with a generic summary.
- [ ] The paper uses “least-bad window,” not “peak.”
- [ ] The paper uses “better of two bad options,” not “good investment.”
- [ ] The paper does not use the word “obviously.”

**Compliance**
- [ ] Body is 1,000–1,500 words.
- [ ] Bibliography is separate and has at least 10 sources.
- [ ] At least half the sources are period primary.
- [ ] At least two sources are non-English.
- [ ] Every source has a direct link to the document, not a homepage.
- [ ] The repo contains the paper, the Makefile, and the notes.
- [ ] The notes are at least twice the length of the paper.

## Part 5: A template for the refutation paragraph

Do not paste this verbatim; use it as a structure.

> One might object that [the VOC’s 18% dividend / the EIC’s monopoly / the lack of a price series] undermines the argument. [State the objection in its strongest form.] The objection has force, but it does not overturn the conclusion. [Concede the part that is true.] However, [introduce the evidence that qualifies it]. [Explain why the balance of evidence still supports the thesis.] The upshot is not that [the original claim] is false, but that [the revised, more precise claim] is better supported.

**Example of the move (paraphrase, not a quotation):** “One might object that the VOC’s 18% dividend makes it the better investment. The objection has force if one looks only at the yield. But the yield was funded by borrowing, the shares were illiquid, and the long-run return was lower than the EIC’s. The upshot is not that the dividend was irrelevant, but that it was compensation for risk rather than a sign of health.”
