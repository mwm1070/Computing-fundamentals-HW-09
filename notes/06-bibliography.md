# Bibliography

**How to use this file.** This is a Chicago-style bibliography with annotations. The assignment asks for at least ten sources, at least half of them period primary, at least two in a language other than English, and a direct link to each document. The list below has fourteen entries: eight primary (seven if you exclude the modern Van Dam edition) and six secondary. Five are in a language other than English. You do not have to cite every entry in the paper, but you should cite at least ten, and every entry you cite should appear here with a direct link.

**A note on “period primary.”** I count a source as period primary if it was written or printed in the period and is used as evidence of contemporary practice or opinion. The 1602 Octrooi and the 1688 De la Vega are earlier than 1725–50, but they are primary evidence for the institutional and market context. The 1727, 1730, and 1749 pamphlets and the 1741 Van Imhoff memorandum are in or near the window. Savary’s 1742 dictionary is in the window. Van Dam was written in 1693–1701 but printed in a modern scholarly edition; if your instructor insists on a period print, drop Van Dam, and the primary ratio is still 7/13.

## Primary sources

**7. De la Vega, Joseph. *Confusión de Confusiones: Diálogos Curiosos entre un Filósofo Agudo, un Mercader Discreto, y un Accionista Erudito.* Amsterdam, 1688. Koninklijke Bibliotheek copy. https://archive.org/details/ned-kbn-all-00002508-001.**

The classic contemporary account of the Amsterdam bourse, in Spanish. It describes the business of shares — their origin, etymology, reality, game, and entanglement — and captures the speculative culture of the market. Use it for the claim that the VOC was trading in a genuinely modern securities market by the 1680s, and for the “confusion of confusions” image. Do not use it for 1725–50 prices.

**8. States General of the Netherlands. *Octrooi van de VOC* [Charter of the Dutch East India Company]. March 20, 1602. Nationaal Archief, The Hague, 1.04.02, inv. 1. https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/onderwijs/bronnenbox/octrooi-voor-de-voc-1602. Dutch transcription: https://nl.wikisource.org/wiki/Octrooi_van_de_VOC.**

The charter that created the VOC’s 21-year monopoly and its sovereign-like powers. Article XXXIV is the monopoly clause; article XXXV grants the Company the right to make treaties, build forts, appoint governors and judges, raise troops, and wage war. The Nationaal Archief’s educational page adds the modern commentary that the Company operated “in effect as a sovereign state in Asia” and that the charter provided the legal basis for slavery and the Banda genocide. Cite the charter for the legal claims and the Nationaal Archief for the interpretation.

**9. Van Imhoff, Gustaaf Willem. *Considérations sur l’état présent de la Compagnie Hollandoise des Indes Orientales, relativement à sa navigation, à son commerce & à son gouvernement, & sur les moyens de remédier aux causes de sa décadence.* 1741. In J. P. J. Du Bois, *Vies des gouverneurs généraux, avec l’abrégé de l’histoire des établissemens hollandois aux Indes Orientales.* The Hague: Pierre de Hondt, 1763. https://archive.org/details/viesdesgouverne00imhogoog.**

The VOC Governor-General’s insider diagnosis of decline, in French. The opening pages identify three causes: over-extended possessions, falling Eastern goods prices in Europe, and demoralized or corrupt employees. Use it as the primary anchor for the claim that the VOC was already declining before 1750. The French edition is a 1763 translation of a 1741 memorandum; note the date if your instructor requires a period print.

**10. Van Dam, Pieter. *Beschryvinge van de Oostindische Compagnie.* 1693–1701. Digital edition, Huygens Institute for the History of the Netherlands. https://resources.huygens.knaw.nl/retroboeken/vandam/.**

A detailed description of the VOC’s organization, governance, trade, and operations, written from inside the Company. Use it for the six chambers, the Heren XVII, and the Company’s combination of trade and sovereignty. The digital edition is modern, not a period print. If you need a strict period primary, drop this entry; the primary ratio remains above half.

**11. [Proprietor in the Company’s Stock]. *A Letter to the Chairman of the East-India Company: In Which the Facts Contained in the Memorials to the Court of Directors, and the Proceedings of the Last General Court, upon the Answer of the Court of Directors to Those Memorials, Are Fully Stated and Shown.* London, 1727. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-letter-to-the-chairman_proprietor-in-the-compan_1727.**

A period pamphlet by an EIC shareholder defending the directors against critics. Use it for EIC investor discourse, governance, and the shareholder’s bottom line. The quotation “what is the only point to the Proprietors, any Loss to the Company” (p. 25) is useful for the investment section. The pamphlet is biased toward the directors; do not treat it as a neutral audit.

**12. *A Collection of Papers Relating to the East India Trade: Wherein Are Shewn the Disadvantages to a Nation, by Confining Any Trade to a Corporation with a Joint-Stock.* London: J. Walthoe, 1730. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-collection-of-papers-r_1730.**

A free-trade critique of the EIC’s joint-stock monopoly. The introduction argues that a chartered corporation with an exclusive trade is “to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly.” Use it for the ethical alternative and for the claim that the EIC was a monopoly even though it was legal. The pamphlet is a polemic, not a neutral analysis.

**13. [A Director]. *Three Letters to the Proprietors of Stock in the East-India Company, Relative to the Question to Be Ballotted for on Wednesday, January 3d, 1749.* London: M. Cooper, 1749. https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_three-letters-to-the-pro_director_1749.**

A period pamphlet by a dissenting EIC director, printed for the proprietors’ ballot of January 3, 1749. The ballot concerned the government’s proposal to reduce the interest on £3.2 million of debt. Use it for the cash-out window: it shows that EIC proprietors were actively voting on the Company’s capital structure in 1749. It is not a share-price series and does not prove a peak.

**14. Savary des Bruslons, Jacques. *Dictionnaire universel de commerce, contenant tout ce qui concerne le commerce qui se fait dans les quatre parties du monde.* Vol. 1. Paris, 1742. https://archive.org/details/b30450470_0001.**

A period French commercial dictionary with entries on companies, shares (*actions*), and trade. Use it for period definitions and a French perspective on company stock. It is optional; the paper already has four non-English sources without it. Do not rely on it for quantitative claims.

## Secondary sources

**1. Zheng, Haiyang. “Reasons for the Decline of the Dutch East India Company.” *Modern Management Forum* 4, no. 3 (2020): 110–114. https://doi.org/10.18686/mmf.v4i3.2505.**

A compact synthesis of the standard VOC-decline case: high salary costs, corruption, monopoly rigidity, the shift from spices to tea and textiles, and dividends funded by borrowing. The key quotation is on p. 113: “Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist.” Use it for the VOC’s cost structure and the dividend trap. It says little about the EIC, so pair it with Nogués-Marco.

**2. Nogués-Marco, Pilar, and Camila Vam Malle-Sabouret. “East India Bonds, 1718–1763: Early Exotic Derivatives and London Market Efficiency.” *European Review of Economic History* 11, no. 3 (2007): 367–394. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1361491607002055.**

The best source for the EIC’s cost of capital. The India bonds were nominally short-term but rolled over into long-term debt with embedded call and put options; the coupon fell to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737. The London market was integrated and capable of pricing sophisticated options. Use it for the comparative investment argument. Note: the publisher may block automated access to the DOI; the local PDF in your Research folder is the working copy.

**3. Phung, Thanh Ngoc Thi. “Dutch East India Company’s Investor Behavior after the First Financial Bubble 1720.” Master’s thesis, Erasmus University Rotterdam, 2013. https://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=131020.**

A quantitative thesis using hand-collected VOC ledger data. It finds a structural break in VOC trading volume after 1720 and liquidity at critically low levels for nearly a decade. Use it for the illiquidity argument and the book-entry nature of VOC shares. It covers 1704–1729, not the whole 1725–50 period; do not overstate its reach.

**4. Rousseau, Peter L. “Historical Perspectives on Financial Development and Economic Growth.” NBER Working Paper 9333, November 2002. https://www.nber.org/papers/w9333.**

A macro-historical account of financial development in the Dutch Republic, England, the United States, and Japan. It describes the VOC’s 2,167 shares at 3,000 guilders par, the emergence of the Amsterdam secondary market, and the Bank of Amsterdam’s support for the stock market. Use it for the “Amsterdam was first” point and then pivot to London’s catch-up. Note: Rousseau dates the Bank of Amsterdam to 1602; the conventional date is 1609.

**5. Price, Gregory N., and Warren Whatley. “The Transatlantic Slave Trade, the South Sea Company and the Financial Revolution in Great Britain.” Working paper, December 27, 2018. https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2019/preliminary/paper/i3FRDG5N. Revised version published as “Did Profitable Slave Trading Enable the Expansion of Empire? The Asiento de Negros, the South Sea Company and the Financial Revolution in Great Britain.” *Cliometrica* 15, no. 3 (2021): 675–718. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11698-020-00219-w.**

A quantitative study of the South Sea Company’s slave trading and its role in the British financial revolution. The authors estimate that in months when a Company slave ship departed, the monthly risk-adjusted return on South Sea stock rose by 7.8%. Use it for the ethics section: the same financial system that gave the EIC cheap capital was entangled with slavery and monopoly. It is not an EIC study; do not imply that the EIC was the Asiento holder.

**6. Taylor, Bryan. *Five Financial Eras: How Financial Markets Transformed the World.* Charlottesville, VA: CFA Institute Research Foundation, 2026. https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/foundation/2026/five-financial-eras. PDF: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/docs/research-reports/rf_taylor_fivefinancialeras_online.pdf.**

A long-run financial history with return data. The key pages are 33–34: 1720–1763 stock returns were 2.94% a year in the United Kingdom and 1.18% in the Netherlands; French and Dutch shares declined between 1721 and 1763; markets hit new lows in 1762; the VOC issued debt rather than new shares. Use it to refute the “1748–50 peak” claim and to support the “least-bad window” formulation. The figures are market-level, not company-level.

## A note on links and access

- All links above were checked and return the document described, except the Nogués-Marco DOI, which returns a 403 to automated requests because Cambridge University Press blocks bots. The DOI is correct; open it in a browser or use the local PDF.
- The archive.org links go to the document pages, not to the archive’s homepage. The PDFs can be downloaded from the “Download Options” panel on each page.
- The Nationaal Archief link is a teaching page, not the raw scan. It includes a transcription of the monopoly clause and a modern commentary. For a stronger citation, use the inventory reference (1.04.02, inv. 1) and the archive’s scan.
- The Van Dam link is a digital edition, not a period print. If your instructor requires a period primary, replace it with another in-window source or drop it; the primary ratio remains above half.

## Suggested in-text citation style

Use author-date or Chicago notes. For example:

- (Zheng 2020, 113)
- (Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret 2007, 371)
- (Van Imhoff 1741/1763, 3–4)
- (*Octrooi van de VOC* 1602, art. XXXIV)
- (*Three Letters to the Proprietors* 1749, 22)

If you use footnotes, keep them short and put the full citation in the bibliography. Do not use “ibid.” across more than one note; it is error-prone.
