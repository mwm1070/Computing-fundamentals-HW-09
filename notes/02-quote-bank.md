# Quote Bank

**How to use this file.** Each entry gives the exact quotation, the citation, a translation if the source is not in English, and a note on what the quote can and cannot prove. Do not paste a quotation into the paper without checking it against the page image. Where the OCR is corrupt, I have modernized the spelling and said so.

---

## 1. The VOC’s dividend trap

> “Shareholders have long been numbed by the dividend of average 18% annual. To pay dividends regularly, the money was borrowed and the company continued to exist.”

**Citation:** Zheng, “Reasons for the Decline of the Dutch East India Company,” p. 113.

**Context:** Zheng is explaining why the VOC’s shareholders did not revolt despite the Company’s deteriorating finances. The dividend was not paid out of current profits; it was financed by borrowing.

**How to use it:** This is the single best quote for the paper’s central paradox: the VOC looked like a high-yield investment, but the yield was a symptom of decline, not strength. Pair it with Taylor’s long-run return figures to show that the 18% dividend did not translate into a high total return.

**Caution:** Zheng does not give a year-by-year dividend series. Do not say the Company paid exactly 18% every year. Say “an average of about 18%,” as Zheng does.

## 2. The VOC as a colonial government

> “VOC was not only an enterprise. It was also a colonial government with a large army and management.”

**Citation:** Zheng, p. 110.

**Context:** Zheng is describing the Company’s fixed costs. By 1750 it had about 35,000 employees, most of them military officers and soldiers.

**How to use it:** Use it to explain why the VOC could not match the EIC’s cost of capital. The Company carried the overhead of a state — forts, troops, garrisons, and a colonial administration — while its monopoly rents were shrinking.

**Caution:** The quote is from a secondary source, not a primary one. If you want a primary anchor for the same point, use the Octrooi (art. XXXV) and Van Imhoff’s discussion of over-extension.

## 3. The monopoly critique

> “By the 18th century, the disadvantages of monopoly had exposed very obvious [sic]. While British private firms had shown the flexible ability to response the market in Asia, especially in India, VOC still stuck to old-fashioned, unwieldy strategy.”

**Citation:** Zheng, p. 111.

**Context:** Zheng is arguing that the VOC’s monopoly model made it rigid, while British private traders and the EIC adapted to the shift from spices to textiles and tea.

**How to use it:** This is a useful bridge between the VOC and EIC sections. The EIC was also a monopoly, but it operated in a more competitive British commercial ecosystem and had access to cheaper capital.

**Caution:** The grammar is Zheng’s, not yours. Quote it with `[sic]` or paraphrase it. Do not imply that the EIC was a free-trade institution; it was not. The 1730 pamphlet makes that clear.

---

## 4. The trade shift

> “The spices and pepper trade was replaced by the cotton and tea trade.”

**Citation:** Zheng, p. 112.

**Context:** Zheng gives the numbers: pepper and spices were 75% of VOC imports around 1620, 41% by 1670, and 23% by 1700; cotton fabrics were 36% in 1670 and 55% in 1700. The VOC was the largest Chinese tea trafficker before the 1760s, but the EIC overtook it by the 1770s.

**How to use it:** Use it to explain why the VOC’s old monopoly rents disappeared. The Company was slow to shift from the spice islands to the tea and textile trades, while the EIC had direct access to Guangzhou and a more diversified portfolio.

**Caution:** Do not overstate the speed of the shift. The VOC remained a major trader; it did not collapse overnight. The paper’s claim is relative decline, not sudden failure.

## 5. The EIC’s cheap, flexible debt

> “Although the India bonds were nominally short-term debts, they were in practice rolled over and thus became equivalent to long-term bonds.”

**Citation:** Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, “East India Bonds,” p. 371.

**Context:** The India bonds were the EIC’s main borrowing instrument. They carried coupons of 3–6%, were repayable by the Company on six months’ notice, and were receivable by the Company as cash in payment for goods at its sales. They functioned as long-term bonds with embedded options.

**How to use it:** This is the core of the comparative investment case. The EIC could borrow cheaply and flexibly from a deep London market. The VOC, by contrast, was issuing debt to pay dividends and its shares had become illiquid.

**Caution:** The quote is about bonds, not equity. Do not use it to claim that EIC shares were low-risk. It supports the claim that the EIC’s *cost of capital* was lower.

## 6. The bond contract

> “The East India Company’s Bonds are payable [by the Company] upon six Months Notice and the Company receives them as Cash in Payment for Goods bought at their Sales, when six Months Interest is due upon them.”

**Citation:** Massie, 1750, pp. 25–26, quoted in Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371.

**Context:** This is a contemporary description of the India bond. It shows that the bond was not a simple loan; it was a hybrid instrument that circulated as cash within the Company’s sales.

**How to use it:** Use it if you want a period voice to show that the EIC’s financial engineering was sophisticated. It also shows that the Company’s debt was embedded in its commercial operations, not separate from them.

**Caution:** The quotation is from a secondary source quoting a primary source. If you can, cite Massie directly; otherwise cite “quoted in Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371.”

## 7. The coupon falls

> “The coupon was reduced to 4% in 1728 and to 3% in 1737.”

**Citation:** Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 371, citing Dickson 1967, pp. 472–473.

**Context:** The reduction in the coupon is evidence that the EIC’s credit improved over the period. Investors were willing to accept a lower yield because the Company’s paper was safe and liquid.

**How to use it:** This is a concrete, dated fact for the 1725–50 window. It shows that the EIC’s cost of capital fell while the VOC’s finances deteriorated.

**Caution:** The coupon is not the same as the yield; the bonds traded near par because of the embedded options. Do not say “the EIC borrowed at 3%” without explaining that this was the coupon on a callable/putable bond.

## 8. The integrated London market

> “After examining certain features peculiar to India bonds overlooked by previous authors, we make it clear that the London domestic market was perfectly integrated and that investors were capable of handling and pricing sophisticated options.”

**Citation:** Nogués-Marco and Vam Malle-Sabouret, p. 367 (abstract).

**Context:** The authors are correcting earlier scholars who thought the London market was inefficient. They show that once the options embedded in India bonds are priced correctly, the market looks integrated.

**How to use it:** Use it to support the claim that the EIC had access to a sophisticated, liquid capital market by the 1720s–1750s. This is a key part of the “EIC was the better investment” argument.

**Caution:** “Perfectly integrated” is the authors’ strong claim. Do not extend it to the whole British economy or to all securities. The point is specific to the London market for EIC paper.

## 9. The VOC’s liquidity collapse

> “There was a significant break in trading volume of VOC after the year 1720 and liquidity for the stock remained at a critically low level for nearly a decade afterwards, implying investors’ significantly lower demand and willingness to trade.”

**Citation:** Phung, “Dutch East India Company’s Investor Behavior,” Abstract, p. 4.

**Context:** Phung uses hand-collected VOC ledger data to show that the Amsterdam market for VOC shares thinned dramatically after the 1720 bubble. The Company did not issue new shares for more than a decade.

**How to use it:** This is the best evidence that the VOC was not a liquid investment in the 1720s and 1730s. It supports the “exit” argument: an investor who wanted to sell VOC stock in 1725–50 faced a thin market and high implicit costs.

**Caution:** The thesis covers 1704–1729, not the whole 1725–50 period. Do not say liquidity was equally bad in 1749; say it had collapsed after 1720 and had not fully recovered.

---

## 10. The VOC’s paperless shares

> “The VOC never issued stock certificates so the only evidence of an investor’s share ownership was the balance on the account under his name in the ledger of the VOC.”

**Citation:** Phung, p. 17.

**Context:** VOC shares were book-entry claims, not bearer certificates. This made trading dependent on the Company’s ledger and its clerks.

**How to use it:** Use it to explain why the VOC’s market was less liquid and less transparent than the London market for EIC shares. It also helps explain why the Company could withhold information from shareholders.

**Caution:** Book-entry ownership is not inherently backward; modern securities are also mostly book-entry. The point is that the VOC’s system was not supported by a deep, competitive market.

## 11. The first modern securities market

> “The VOC was capitalized with 2,167 shares at a par value of 3,000 fl. each, and the owners could liquidate their stakes through the Company once every ten years. But when the Directors repudiated this provision at the end of the first decade, those wishing to liquidate needed a secondary market. It was in this climate that shares and futures began to trade on the Amsterdam bourse — the world’s first modern securities market.”

**Citation:** Rousseau, “Historical Perspectives on Financial Development,” p. 3.

**Context:** Rousseau is describing the origins of the Amsterdam stock market. The VOC’s attempt to limit liquidity inadvertently created the secondary market.

**How to use it:** Use it in the introduction or the “two companies, two constitutions” section. It establishes that the VOC was at the center of the first modern capital market, which makes its later decline more striking.

**Caution:** The quote is about the early seventeenth century, not 1725–50. Use it as context, and then show that the Dutch advantage eroded.

## 12. Slavery and the British financial revolution

> “In months when at least one South Sea Company slave ship departed London for Africa and the South Seas, the monthly risk-adjusted return on South Sea Company stock increased by an estimated 7.8%.”

**Citation:** Price and Whatley, “The Transatlantic Slave Trade,” p. 4.

**Context:** The South Sea Company held the Asiento, the monopoly right to supply enslaved Africans to the Spanish Empire, from 1713 to 1743. The authors argue that slave trading created substantial positive risk-adjusted returns and helped the government refinance its debt.

**How to use it:** This is the ethics section’s strongest quantitative evidence. It shows that the British financial revolution — the system that gave the EIC cheap capital — was entangled with slavery and monopoly.

**Caution:** The 7.8% figure is a monthly risk-adjusted return for South Sea stock, not an annual return, and not an EIC figure. Do not imply that the EIC was the Asiento holder. The connection is systemic, not identical.

---

## 13. The cost of British debt

> “The market value of the national debt approached par and the cost of long-term government borrowing declined from 8.7% to approximately 3%.”

**Citation:** Price and Whatley, p. 3.

**Context:** The authors are describing the British financial revolution of 1711–1725. The decline in borrowing costs was the result of credible commitment, better institutions, and the South Sea Company’s debt-for-equity swaps.

**How to use it:** Use it to explain why the EIC could borrow so cheaply by the 1720s–1750s. The EIC’s India bonds were part of the same low-interest, liquid market.

**Caution:** The 8.7% → 3% figure is for long-term government debt, not EIC bonds. Use it as context for the broader financial revolution, not as a direct EIC statistic.

---

## 14. The secular bear market

> “Shares in French Compagnie des Indes Orientales and Dutch VOC shares gradually declined between 1721 and 1763. The VOC found it more difficult to make profits in the 1700s. It chose to issue more debt rather than new shares, eventually creating a debt load that would force it into bankruptcy.”

**Citation:** Taylor, *Five Financial Eras*, p. 34.

**Context:** Taylor is describing the “Mercantilist Economy” of 1720–1763. The period was a secular bear market for equities in England, France, and the Netherlands. The VOC’s debt strategy made its shares even less attractive.

**How to use it:** This is the key quote for the cash-out section. It refutes the old paper’s “cash out in 1748–50 at the post-war peak” claim. There was no peak; there was a long decline.

**Caution:** Taylor’s figures are market-level, not company-level. Do not say “VOC shares fell every year from 1721 to 1763.” Say “the long-run trend was downward.”

---

## 15. The 1762 lows

> “Stock markets in England, France, and the Netherlands all hit new lows at the beginning of 1762. There were no major bull markets for four decades as most investors chose safe government bonds over risky equities.”

**Citation:** Taylor, p. 34.

**Context:** The Seven Years’ War (1756–63) and the earlier wars depressed equity prices. The post-war period did not bring a sustained recovery.

**How to use it:** Use it to show that an investor who held EIC or VOC stock through the 1750s would have suffered. The late 1740s were a window to exit, not a peak to celebrate.

**Caution:** The “four decades” claim is about major bull markets, not about every year. Do not overstate it.

## 16. The Amsterdam bourse

> “Confusión de Confusiones. Diálogos Curiosos entre un Filósofo Agudo, un Mercader Discreto, y un Accionista Erudito. Descubriendo el negocio de las Acciones, su origen, su ethimología, su realidad, su juego, y su enredo.”

**Citation:** De la Vega, *Confusión de Confusiones* (Amsterdam, 1688), title page. Original spelling “ethimología.”

**Translation:** “Confusion of Confusions. Curious Dialogues between a Sharp Philosopher, a Discreet Merchant, and a Learned Shareholder. Discovering the business of shares, their origin, their etymology, their reality, their game, and their entanglement.”

**Context:** De la Vega’s book is the classic contemporary account of the Amsterdam stock market. It describes the market as a place of rumor, strategy, and entanglement, not just fundamental value.

**How to use it:** Use it in the introduction to establish that the VOC was trading in a genuinely modern securities market by the 1680s. The title itself is a useful rhetorical hook: the first modern stock market was already a “confusion of confusions.”

**Caution:** The book is literary and moralizing, not statistical. Do not cite it for 1725–50 prices.

## 17. The VOC charter

> “Ende op dat het voornemen van deze Compagnie met meerder vrugt mag uitgevoert worden … zoo hebben wy de voorsz Compagnie geoctroyeert ende geaccordeert, octroyeren ende accorderen mits dezen, dat niemant, van wat qualiteit ofte conditie die zy, anders dan die van de voorsz Compagnie uit deze vereenigde Landen zal mogen vaaren, binnen den tyd van 21 jaaren eerstkomende, beginnende met dezen jaare 1602. incluis, Beoosten de kaap de Bonne Esperance, ofte door de straat van Magellanes, op de verbeurte van de schepen en goederen…”

**Citation:** *Octrooi van de VOC*, art. XXXIV, 20 March 1602.

**Translation:** “…that no one of whatever quality or condition, other than those of the aforesaid Company from these United Provinces, may sail within the time of 21 years next coming, beginning this year 1602, east of the Cape of Good Hope or through the Strait of Magellan, on pain of forfeiture of the ships and goods…”

**Context:** This is the monopoly clause. It is the legal foundation of the VOC’s exclusive trade.

**How to use it:** Use it to show that the VOC was a chartered monopoly, not a free-market firm. The phrase “on pain of forfeiture of the ships and goods” is vivid and quotable.

**Caution:** The charter is from 1602, not 1725–50. Use it for the legal structure, not the later finances.

## 18. The VOC as a sovereign

> “De VOC mag van de Staten-Generaal bovendien gebieden met geweld innemen en deze vervolgens besturen en exploiteren. … De VOC opereert in feite als soevereine (onafhankelijke) staat in Azië.”

**Citation:** Nationaal Archief, “Octrooi voor de VOC (1602),” educational page.

**Translation:** “The VOC was moreover permitted by the States General to seize territories by force and then govern and exploit them. … The VOC operated in effect as a sovereign (independent) state in Asia.”

**Context:** The Nationaal Archief’s page explains the sovereign powers that the charter granted: treaties, forts, courts, troops, and war.

**How to use it:** Use it in the ethics section. The VOC was not just a company; it was a state-like power with the legal right to conquer, govern, and enslave.

**Caution:** This is a modern commentary, not the charter itself. Cite it as the Nationaal Archief’s interpretation, and pair it with the charter articles.

---

## 19. The VOC and slavery

> “Daarmee staat de Staten-Generaal toe dat de VOC mensen die in een ‘rechtvaardige oorlog’ gevangen worden genomen tot slaaf maakt.”

**Citation:** Nationaal Archief, “Octrooi voor de VOC (1602).”

**Translation:** “The States General thus permitted the VOC to enslave people captured in a ‘just war.’”

**Context:** The page explains that the Company’s sovereign powers included the right to wage war, and that the law of war allowed enslavement.

**How to use it:** Use it to make the ethical stakes concrete. The VOC’s monopoly was not a neutral commercial arrangement; it was backed by violence and slavery.

**Caution:** Do not imply that the EIC was innocent. The EIC was also a chartered monopoly embedded in a coercive empire. The paper’s ethical argument is about the structure, not a contest of moral purity.

## 20. Van Imhoff’s diagnosis

> “Sans contredit la Compagnie Hollandoise des Indes Orientales n’est pas aujourd’hui dans cet état florissant où elle étoit autrefois. On en allègue différentes causes directes & indirectes, que l’on peut réduire à trois principales: 1°. la trop grande étendue de ses possessions dans les Indes, ce qui en rend l’administration moins aisée & plus sujette à des inconvéniens. 2°. L’abondance excessive des productions de l’Orient, & par conséquent la diminution de leur prix en Europe: enfin 3°. le relâchement de zèle & d’attention de la part des Employés au Service de la Compagnie…”

**Citation:** Van Imhoff, *Considérations*, pp. 3–4. Modernized from the OCR.

**Translation:** “The Dutch East India Company is undoubtedly not today in the flourishing state it once was. Various direct and indirect causes are alleged, which can be reduced to three main ones: (1) the too-great extent of its possessions in the Indies, which makes administration less easy and more prone to problems; (2) the excessive abundance of Eastern produce, and consequently the fall in its price in Europe; finally (3) the relaxation of zeal and attention on the part of the employees in the Company’s service…”

**Context:** Van Imhoff was the VOC’s Governor-General. He wrote this memorandum in 1741, in the middle of the period under study. It is an insider’s diagnosis, not a hostile outsider’s polemic.

**How to use it:** This is the primary-source anchor for the “VOC was already declining before 1750” argument. Use the three causes to structure the VOC section.

**Caution:** The French edition was printed in 1763, though the memorandum was written in 1741. If your instructor requires a period print, note the date carefully.

## 21. The EIC’s defender

> “And that this is a beneficial Trade, is evident to any Man acquainted with the Imports and Exports of the Company.”

**Citation:** *A Letter to the Chairman of the East-India Company* (1727), p. 5.

**Context:** The author, a proprietor, is defending the EIC against critics who accused the directors of mismanagement.

**How to use it:** Use it to show that EIC shareholders publicly defended the Company and its trade. It is a period voice, not a modern judgment.

**Caution:** The pamphlet is a defence of the directors, so it is biased. Do not use it as proof that the trade was beneficial; use it as evidence of investor discourse.

## 22. The monopoly accusation

> “By this means they made great advantage of their Knowledge, by the Monopoly of those Commodities.”

**Citation:** *A Letter to the Chairman* (1727), p. 22.

**Context:** The author is paraphrasing the critics’ charge that the directors used their inside knowledge of the September sale to buy goods cheaply. It is an accusation, not a confession.

**How to use it:** Use it carefully. It shows that EIC shareholders were worried about insider dealing and monopoly power. It does not prove that the directors were guilty.

**Caution:** Do not quote it as if the author were admitting misconduct. The author is defending the directors and summarizing the charge in order to rebut it.

## 23. The proprietor’s bottom line

> “What is the only point to the Proprietors, any Loss to the Company, by the misconduct of the Directors?”

**Citation:** *A Letter to the Chairman* (1727), p. 25. Modernized from the OCR.

**Context:** The author is arguing that the only thing that matters to shareholders is whether the Company lost money.

**How to use it:** This is a useful quote for the investment decision. It shows that shareholders cared about profit, not imperial glory or directors’ reputations.

**Caution:** It is a rhetorical question, not a philosophical statement. Do not overinterpret it.

## 24. The free-trade critique

> “That however any Corporation may have the Sanction of Parliament for the carrying on a Trade, exclusive of all other their fellow Subject, yet that notwithstanding, they are to all Intents and Purposes a Monopoly…”

**Citation:** *A Collection of Papers Relating to the East India Trade* (1730), “The Introduction,” p. vi. Modernized from the OCR.

**Context:** The pamphlet is a free-trade critique of the EIC’s joint-stock monopoly. It argues that a parliamentary charter does not make a monopoly legitimate.

**How to use it:** This is the best primary source for the ethical alternative. It gives a period argument that the EIC’s charter was a monopoly even though it was legal, and that private trade was a better alternative.

**Caution:** The pamphlet is a polemic by free-trade interests. It is not a neutral analysis. Use it for the argument, not as proof that private trade was more efficient.

## 25. The 1749 ballot

> “Three Letters to the Proprietors of Stock in the East-India Company, Relative to the Question to Be Ballotted for on Wednesday, January 3d, 1749.”

**Citation:** *Three Letters to the Proprietors* (1749), title page.

**Context:** EIC proprietors were voting on whether to accept the government’s proposal to reduce the interest on £3.2 million of government debt.

**How to use it:** This is the primary anchor for the cash-out section. It shows that in January 1749 — in the middle of the 1745–50 window — EIC proprietors were actively voting on the Company’s capital structure.

**Caution:** The pamphlet is about the interest reduction, not about selling shares. Do not claim it proves a share-price peak.

## 26. The Company’s capital stock

> “…the Debt on the Two First Loans amounting to 3,200,000 l. which is the Company’s Capital Stock…”

**Citation:** *Three Letters to the Proprietors* (1749), p. 22. Modernized from the OCR.

**Context:** The author is explaining the Company’s capital structure. The £3.2 million figure is the capital stock on which the proprietors’ dividends and votes depended.

**How to use it:** Use it to make the 1749 decision concrete. The proprietors were not passive investors; they were voting on the terms of the Company’s debt.

**Caution:** The £3.2 million is the capital stock, not the market value of the Company. Do not confuse the two.
